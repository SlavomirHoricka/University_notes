# Environment–Economy Nexus

How does economic activity depend on the environment, and how does it feed back into it? The opening material answers this question by extending the familiar relationship between firms and households. Nature supplies resources, receives waste, provides services people enjoy directly, and maintains the systems on which life depends. Production and consumption therefore sit inside a wider physical and welfare system. This is the introductory framework for studying the Green Deal's economic, environmental and social impacts. ## Four environmental functions

The slides distinguish four ways in which the environment matters to the economy. These functions can coexist in the same place. The forest example shows the distinction particularly clearly: wood is a resource, while aesthetic, cultural and recreational enjoyment belongs to the separate category of demand services. This comparison is an explanatory synthesis of the examples on slide 2. [[00_Materials/2026_2027/Winter_Semester/Economics_of_Green_Deal/Week_1/00_Intro.pptx|00_Intro.pptx, slide 2]]

### Resources

Resources provide inputs or possibilities for economic activity. Slide 2 distinguishes **non-degradable resources**, with sunshine, wind and waves as examples, from **degradable resources**. Within the latter group it distinguishes renewable resources, such as wood and biomass, from non-renewable or exhaustible resources, such as fossil fuels and minerals. It also lists space as a resource. Keep this source taxonomy when interpreting the later diagram: the slide uses “renewable” for the wood/biomass category and separately lists sunshine, wind and waves as non-degradable. It does not supply a broader taxonomy or a formal definition of “degradable.” [[00_Materials/2026_2027/Winter_Semester/Economics_of_Green_Deal/Week_1/00_Intro.pptx|00_Intro.pptx, slide 2]]

The distinction becomes economically meaningful when resource use is compared with replenishment. An exhaustible resource can be reduced by extraction. For renewable resources, the diagram separately represents harvesting above and below yield or growth, so “renewable” alone does not establish that a particular pattern of use maintains the resource. The relevant question is how fast the resource is used relative to how fast it grows. [[00_Materials/2026_2027/Winter_Semester/Economics_of_Green_Deal/Week_1/00_Intro.pptx|00_Intro.pptx, slides 2 and 4]]

### Sinks and assimilative capacity

A **sink** is a place where undesirable outputs, including emissions and wastes, may be placed. The source describes this function through **assimilative capacity**: the environment's capacity to receive or assimilate those outputs. The diagram later compares waste with that capacity. This makes waste disposal part of the environment–economy relationship, alongside resource extraction. [[00_Materials/2026_2027/Winter_Semester/Economics_of_Green_Deal/Week_1/00_Intro.pptx|00_Intro.pptx, slides 2 and 4]]

The capacity comparison matters because the diagram assigns different consequences to waste below capacity and waste above capacity. The existence of a sink therefore does not establish that every amount of waste has the same consequences. The source supplies a qualitative threshold comparison; it does not quantify a safe emissions level, a pollutant-specific assimilation rate, or the time needed for assimilation. [[00_Materials/2026_2027/Winter_Semester/Economics_of_Green_Deal/Week_1/00_Intro.pptx|00_Intro.pptx, slide 4]]

### Demand services

The environment also supplies services people value directly. Slide 2 names aesthetic, cultural and recreational services, such as those provided by a forest. In the full diagram, the environmental resource node has a positive amenity connection to utility. Thus the environment contributes to wellbeing through more than goods produced by firms and consumed by households. [[00_Materials/2026_2027/Winter_Semester/Economics_of_Green_Deal/Week_1/00_Intro.pptx|00_Intro.pptx, slides 2 and 4]]

**Utility**, denoted by $U$, is the diagram's representation of wellbeing or benefit. The source identifies utility flows but gives no numerical utility scale, utility function, or valuation method. The positive amenity arrow establishes a direction of effect in this schematic framework; it does not report a measured monetary benefit. [[00_Materials/2026_2027/Winter_Semester/Economics_of_Green_Deal/Week_1/00_Intro.pptx|00_Intro.pptx, slide 4]]

### Global biospheric life-supporting services

The fourth category concerns the systems that support life more generally. The source names the climate system, hydrologic cycle and soil production. These services place the production-and-consumption relationship in a wider biospheric setting. They explain why environmental economics considers systemic conditions as well as individual resource inputs and enjoyable amenities. This final sentence is a synthesis of the category's role in the introductory framework. [[00_Materials/2026_2027/Winter_Semester/Economics_of_Green_Deal/Week_1/00_Intro.pptx|00_Intro.pptx, slide 2]]

## Reading the physical and utility network

![[01_Notes/2026_2027/Winter_Semester/Economics_of_Green_Deal/assets/Intro_Slide_04_Environment_Economy_Network.png]]

The blue highlight locates production and consumption within the wider network. Plus and minus signs show qualitative directions of effect.* [[00_Materials/2026_2027/Winter_Semester/Economics_of_Green_Deal/Week_1/00_Intro.pptx|00_Intro.pptx, slide 4]]

The diagram also contains a lower-case $r$ on the return path from waste toward resources. The source's printed legend does not define $r$. The path can be identified visually as a material return path, but this note does not assign it a verified name, rate, or equation. [[00_Materials/2026_2027/Winter_Semester/Economics_of_Green_Deal/Week_1/00_Intro.pptx|00_Intro.pptx, slide 4, lower-left return path and legend]]

### Resource use and replenishment

The main horizontal physical path runs from natural resources ($R$) through production ($P$) to consumption ($C$). The resource branch separates exhaustible resources ($ER$) and renewable resources ($RR$). On the exhaustible branch, the displayed case $h>y$ connects with a negative sign. On the renewable branch, $h>y$ has a negative sign, whereas $h<y$ has a positive sign. [[00_Materials/2026_2027/Winter_Semester/Economics_of_Green_Deal/Week_1/00_Intro.pptx|00_Intro.pptx, slide 4, left resource branches]]

These inequalities compare harvesting with yield or growth. If harvesting exceeds growth, use runs ahead of replenishment and the diagram represents a reduction of the resource. If harvesting is below growth on the renewable branch, replenishment can exceed removal and the diagram represents a positive effect on that resource. For this interpretation, $h$ and $y$ must refer to comparable quantities over the same time interval. This comparability is a mathematical requirement of reading the inequality, rather than a separate numerical assumption supplied by the slide. The diagram does not specify a growth function, units, a time horizon, or an equality case $h=y$. It therefore supports directional reasoning, rather than a calculation of a sustainable harvest level. [[00_Materials/2026_2027/Winter_Semester/Economics_of_Green_Deal/Week_1/00_Intro.pptx|00_Intro.pptx, slide 4]]

### Waste and capacity

Solid paths from the resource, production and consumption parts of the diagram join the waste node $W$. Waste then connects to assimilative capacity $A$. Two cases appear below it: $W<A$ and $W>A$. The $W<A$ branch returns to resources with a positive sign. The $W>A$ branch returns with a negative sign and also produces the dashed negative amenity connection to utility. [[00_Materials/2026_2027/Winter_Semester/Economics_of_Green_Deal/Week_1/00_Intro.pptx|00_Intro.pptx, slide 4, waste/capacity branches]]

Read $W<A$ as waste remaining below the capacity represented in this schematic model, and $W>A$ as waste exceeding it. The latter case makes the feedback explicit: undesirable output can impair the environmental resource base and reduce utility. As with harvesting and growth, the comparison requires a common basis of measurement. The slide gives no units or detailed physical law and does not discuss the equality case $W=A$. It also does not establish that all waste below capacity is harmless in every setting. The diagram's positive and negative arrows represent its qualitative framework, not a universal empirical emissions rule. [[00_Materials/2026_2027/Winter_Semester/Economics_of_Green_Deal/Week_1/00_Intro.pptx|00_Intro.pptx, slide 4]]

### Several paths to wellbeing

Consumption has a positive dashed link to utility. Natural resources also connect to utility through the upper “Positive amenity (+)” path, while waste exceeding capacity connects through the lower-right “Negative amenity (−)” path. These distinct paths show why production or consumption alone cannot describe the whole environment–economy relationship: the same network contains benefits from consumption, benefits from environmental amenities, and losses associated with environmental pressures. This is an explanatory reading of the diagram, not a quantitative welfare formula. [[00_Materials/2026_2027/Winter_Semester/Economics_of_Green_Deal/Week_1/00_Intro.pptx|00_Intro.pptx, slide 4]]

Slide 5 overlays the course's economic questions on the physical network. **Resources** appears beside the resource branches. **Growth and sustainability** appears above the production-and-consumption relationship. **Externality** appears along feedback paths. **Regulation** appears around the production/waste part of the network. **Valuation** appears beside the amenity connections to utility. An explanatory synthesis is to use this network as a sequence of questions. What resources support economic activity? Does resource use outrun replenishment? How much waste reaches the environment relative to its capacity? Which pathways change wellbeing, and how should those changes be assessed? The source's regulation and valuation labels point toward these policy and assessment questions. Formal externality theory and instrument comparisons belong to later material and are not supplied by these introductory diagrams. ## What the introductory framework establishes

The economy depends on resources and wider environmental services, while production and consumption generate waste and feedback to the environment and utility. The crucial conditions in the source diagram compare harvest with growth and waste with assimilative capacity. These conditions make resource renewal and environmental capacity part of the analysis. The diagram offers a conceptual foundation for later policy assessment, while leaving quantitative models, measurements and climate science unspecified.

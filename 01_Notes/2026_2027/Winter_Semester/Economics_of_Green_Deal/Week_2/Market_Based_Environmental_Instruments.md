# Market Based Environmental Instruments

Once an externality is identified, policy must determine both the environmental objective and how to achieve it. A market based instrument puts a price or tradable constraint on emissions so that firms can choose their own response. The lecture separates **static efficiency**, **dynamic incentives** and **environmental effectiveness**: cheap compliance, innovation and achievement of a target answer different questions. The [[01_Notes/2026_2027/Winter_Semester/Economics_of_Green_Deal/Week_2/Externalities_and_Welfare_Optimum|welfare-optimum note]] establishes why the private signal needs correction; this note explains the available instruments. ## Four instrument families

![[01_Notes/2026_2027/Winter_Semester/Economics_of_Green_Deal/assets/Week2_Instrument_Families_Slide31.png]]

*Lecture physical PDF p. 31, based on Böchler (2012). Direct regulation, market based, cooperative and informational instruments are separate families; the attached OECD pointer is not an additional ingested source.* [[00_Materials/2026_2027/Winter_Semester/Economics_of_Green_Deal/Week_2/01_Regulation_Externality-1.pdf#page=31|Lecture, physical PDF p. 31]]

Direct regulation includes emission/technical standards, bans on toxic substances and land-planning measures. Price based instruments include taxes, direct subsidies, feed-in tariffs and bonus/malus systems; quantity based instruments include tradable permits and green certificates. Cooperative approaches include voluntary agreements, roundtables, mediation and forest certification. Information instruments include ecolabelling, communication campaigns and education. The classification describes how policy acts; it does not demonstrate that all entries have equal enforceability or welfare effects. ## Tradable rights and flexibility

A firm with allowances above its emissions can sell its surplus to another firm that lacks them. A market price makes saving an allowance valuable even when it was originally allocated free: abatement permits a sale or avoids a purchase. A cap-and-trade system sets an aggregate quota; a baseline-and-credit system issues emission-reduction credits relative to a baseline. The lecture also lists minimum-limit-and-trade arrangements, averaging across similar products (cars/engines), and transferable use/abstraction rights such as fishing quotas. These forms require different baselines and definitions of the tradable right; the source supplies their classification rather than detailed design rules. [[00_Materials/2026_2027/Winter_Semester/Economics_of_Green_Deal/Week_2/01_Regulation_Externality-1.pdf#page=34|Lecture, physical PDF p. 34]]

### Flexibility across time

Budgeting emissions or abstractions over a period allows transfers within that period. **Banking** moves a currently valid permit to a later period; **borrowing** uses a permit from a future period early. Differentiating ordinary operation from short pollution peaks is another listed form of timing flexibility. These arrangements change when compliance occurs, so a total over several years and a short-term peak constraint are different requirements. This distinction is explanatory synthesis of the listed forms; the lecture does not specify discount rates, borrowing limits or safeguards. [[00_Materials/2026_2027/Winter_Semester/Economics_of_Green_Deal/Week_2/01_Regulation_Externality-1.pdf#page=32|Lecture, physical PDF p. 32]]

### Flexibility across space

An **emission bubble** sets a total emissions ceiling for a designated territory with several sources. A **deposition bubble** instead covers sources contributing deposition to a target area. **Offsets** transfer between bubbles while adjusting their ceilings, possibly with weights. **Netting** is a bubble-like arrangement within an industrial facility. The distinction between emitted quantity and deposition at a receptor matters because equal quantities from different locations need not have identical consequences. The source lists the forms but supplies no numerical transport/deposition coefficients. [[00_Materials/2026_2027/Winter_Semester/Economics_of_Green_Deal/Week_2/01_Regulation_Externality-1.pdf#page=33|Lecture, physical PDF p. 33]]

## Why common marginal costs minimize total abatement costs

Let $a_i$ be firm $i$'s abatement, $C_i(a_i)$ its abatement cost and $MAC_i=C_i'(a_i)$ its marginal abatement cost, expressed as money per avoided emission unit. For a fixed total abatement requirement $\bar a$, an explanatory derivation of the lecture's least-cost condition is

$$\min_{a_1,\ldots,a_n}\sum_i C_i(a_i)\quad\text{subject to}\quad\sum_i a_i=\bar a.$$

At a smooth interior optimum, $MAC_i(a_i)=\lambda$ for every firm, where $\lambda$ is the common marginal cost of meeting the target. If one firm instead has higher MAC, shifting a small amount of abatement from it to a lower-MAC firm keeps the total unchanged and lowers cost. This argument assumes comparable emission/abatement units and a target that allows the transfer. Location-specific damage, constraints and transaction costs can restrict it; the lecture does not establish that unrestricted trading of every pollutant is harmless. [[00_Materials/2026_2027/Winter_Semester/Economics_of_Green_Deal/Week_2/01_Regulation_Externality-1.pdf#page=33|Lecture, physical PDF pp. 33–34,44]]

Under a tax $t$ per emitted unit, a firm saves $t$ by avoiding one unit and, at an interior choice, abates until $MAC_i=t$. With an allowance price $p$, it abates and sells/avoids buying an allowance when $MAC_i<p$, while emitting and buying is cheaper when $MAC_i>p$; the equilibrium margin is $MAC_i=p$. A common price thus equalizes marginal costs across unconstrained firms. This is **cost effectiveness for a given target**. It establishes full welfare efficiency only if the target/price also reflects social benefits and damages under the conditions in the optimum model. ## Innovation and environmental effectiveness

Pricing each emission unit gives a continuing reason to seek cheaper avoidance. The lecture illustrates innovation as a shift from $MAC_1$ to $MAC_2$, with greater abatement at the same tax and a reduction in compliance cost. But innovation need not lower MAC at every margin: a technology can lower some parts of the curve while raising others. The source distinguishes this from commonly assumed reductions in average cost at all margins. The welfare or firm gain must therefore follow the actual curves, rather than treating every innovation as a parallel downward shift. [[00_Materials/2026_2027/Winter_Semester/Economics_of_Green_Deal/Week_2/01_Regulation_Externality-1.pdf#page=44|Lecture, physical PDF pp. 44–45]]

![[01_Notes/2026_2027/Winter_Semester/Economics_of_Green_Deal/assets/Week2_Innovation_MAC_Slide45.png]]

*Lecture p. 45: the horizontal Pigouvian tax intersects $MAC_1$ at $A_1$ and $MAC_2$ at $A_2$. The slide discusses the area between marginal curves but also qualifies the commonly used downward-shift picture.* [[00_Materials/2026_2027/Winter_Semester/Economics_of_Green_Deal/Week_2/01_Regulation_Externality-1.pdf#page=45|Lecture, physical PDF p. 45]]

A monitored/enforced ban or direct quantity requirement specifies a target. A tax specifies a rate and leaves the response—and therefore the achieved emission quantity—dependent on behaviour, exemptions and costs. A tradable quantity constraint fixes the covered quantity while leaving its price to the market. Voluntary and information instruments are difficult to enforce or give uncertain responses. These are the lecture's ideal effectiveness comparisons. Clear targets require monitoring, enforcement and appropriate coverage; a cap on covered sources is not by itself proof of an economy-wide emission outcome. The lecture mentions minimizing control mistakes under price-versus-quantity instruments but does not derive an uncertainty-based ranking. [[00_Materials/2026_2027/Winter_Semester/Economics_of_Green_Deal/Week_2/01_Regulation_Externality-1.pdf#page=44|Lecture, physical PDF pp. 44,47]]

## Historical policy examples and chart interpretation

The lecture reports that the US Acid Rain Program followed the 1990 Clean Air Act amendment, began SO₂ trading in 1995 and expanded in 2000. It attributes practicality to relatively few easily monitored polluters and reports emissions more than 50% lower in 2007, three years before the statutory deadline, with compliance costs about one quarter of original predictions. The slide does not state the reference year for that percentage or an identification method separating trading from other influences. Treat these as the supplied lecture's historical descriptive claims, not a new causal estimate or current performance measure. ## What to retain

Equalizing marginal abatement costs economizes on compliance resources, while selecting the social target and achieving it require additional reasoning and enforcement. A price incentive can promote innovation, but the technology response is not mechanically uniform. Interpret ETS quantity, price, coverage, auctions, offsets and revenue as separate metrics with their source dates and units.

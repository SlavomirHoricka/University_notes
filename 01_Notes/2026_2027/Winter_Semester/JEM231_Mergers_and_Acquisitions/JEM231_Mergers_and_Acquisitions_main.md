### Introduction of M&A transaction types:
- Horizontal
	- similar companies merge together for some mutual benefit, such as economies of scale, or the combining of their respective customer bases
	- a PC maker and accessory maker merge to sell products to customers together
- Vertical
	- a company purchases another up or down in the supply chain
	- a laptop maker purchases a company which makes screens for said laptops	- 
- Concentric
	- different products, but same customer bases merge together to sell their products together
	- shoe maker and sock maker merge into one
- Conglomerate
	-  used to be far more common
	- a company buying another to diversify the business
	- may lead to companies to branch out into completely alien segments to their original business

### Sources of synergies
> - **economies of scale**
> 	- most common reason for takeovers
> 	- typically applies to horizontal mergers
> 	- reducing per unit cost by consolidating capacity
> - **complementary resources**
> 	- typically applies to horizontal mergers
> 	- for example a small firm that owns a valuable patent but lacks engineering facility or marketing network is a good target for a cash cow
> - **economies of integration**
> 	- supply chain optimization
> 	- typically applies to vertical mergers
> 	- gain control of suppliers to optimize supply chain or eliminate profit margin
> - **management (in)efficiency**
> 	- acquiring firm replaces “bad” management
> 	- typically hostile

- inefficiently run firms become takeover targets - it is a question of corporate governance, i.e. replacing bad management by acquiring it and inserting your own new management
- introducing anti-takeover provisions may disincentivize management from working hard, because the threat of a hostile takeover is lowered

> - **surplus funds**
> 	- managers of firms with large cash reserves may be reluctant to return money to shareholders
> 	- an acquiring firm may enforce more efficient deployment of that cash
> 	- for example:
> 		- in the 1980s, many oil firms held large cash piles
> 		- falling oil prices made new exploration unprofitable
> 		- this contributed to a wave of takeovers

- quite frequently a large reason for activist shareholders to come into the picture

> - **industry consolidation**
> 	- in industries with too many firms and too much capacity, a wave of M&As forces firms to cut capacity and employment
> 	- examples:
> 		- US banking in the 1980s
> 		- the mobile-phone industry (Vodafone, Nokia, BT)

### Dubious reasons for mergers
> - **diversification**
> 	- intended to reduce business risk in the pure sense
> 	- but investors can diversify more cheaply by combining shares of different firms themselves
> 	- empire building?
> 	- for example:
> 		- Kaiser holding group = Kaiser steel + Kaiser aluminium + Kaiser cement
> 		- all four traded separately, but the holding group was valued below the sum of its parts
> 		- the difference disappeared when Kaiser announced plans to dissolve the holding group
> 	- diversification can still create unique synergies, such as financing in car retail

- the point is that the diversification is a lot more efficient when the shareholders do it themselves
- can also be reasonable in terms of value-creation, for example with car dealerships offering financing for the vehicles they are selling
	- it could be argued that this is complimentary vertical merger, the argument here is that the customer may not buy the car if financing is not offered

> - **boost earnings per share**
> 	- deals that increase EPS are called **accretive deals**; deals that decrease EPS are **dilutive**
> - managers care about **EPS accretion** versus **dilution**
> 	- EPS is widely used to measure the management team's performance, creating pressure to deliver **consistent EPS growth**
> 	- managerial remuneration is also linked to EPS growth
> - the illusion occurs in stock deals when a high-P/E firm buys a low-P/E firm
> 	- it is **independent of the deal's NPV**

- easy to calculate and communicate the EPS
- a deal may be EPS accretive, and have a positive NPV, and vice versa

#### Example: EPS accretion with zero NPV
> - Bidder B buys target T with zero synergies, so the project's NPV is zero.
> 	- **$EPS_B=2$** and **$EPS_T=2$**
> 	- because of the price difference, each target share is exchanged for **$x=\frac{1}{2}$** bidder share

| Item | Bidder B | Target T | Combined firm |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Earnings | 200 | 200 | 400 |
| Shares | 100 | 100 | 150 |
| EPS | 2.00 | 2.00 | 2.67 |
| Share price | 40 | 20 | 40 |
| P/E ratio | 20 | 10 | 15 |
| Value | 4,000 | 2,000 | 6,000 |

> - The firms merely pool earnings, while the combined share count is $100+50=150$.
> 	- therefore **$EPS_{new}=\frac{400}{150}=2.67$**
> - EPS rises even though NPV is zero.

- an example of how EPS can be manipulated
- P/E ratio: !!!
	- the numerator: a *price* of a share should reflect the present value of all future cash flows acquired from owning it
	- the denominator: *current earnings*
	- need to look at growth potential, essentially higher p/e would mean higher growth potential
- exchange ratio - 1 share of T buys $\frac{1}{2}$ share of B

#### Accretive deals
> - The dilution ratio **$x$** is important: the EPS of one target share is spread over $x$ new shares.
> 	- in the example, the EPS of one target share is spread over **$x=\frac{1}{2}$** new shares
> 	- if **$\frac{EPS_T}{x}>EPS_B$**, then **$EPS_{new}>EPS_B$** and the deal is **accretive**, even when **NPV is zero**
> - A deal is accretive if **$\frac{EPS_T}{x}>EPS_B$**.
> - Therefore, it remains accretive while:
>   $$\boldsymbol{x<\frac{EPS_T}{EPS_B}\equiv x_{max}}$$
> - A high bidder P/E implies a low exchange ratio:
>   $$\boldsymbol{x=\frac{p_T}{p_B}=\frac{EPS_T}{EPS_B}\times\frac{P/E_T}{P/E_B}}$$

- definition of accretion !!!
- minimize $x$

#### How the NPV rule fits in
> - Accretion/dilution analysis considers only the effect of the deal on the buyer's EPS.
> 	- it does not show whether the deal has positive NPV or **creates value** for shareholders
> 	- focusing on EPS **creates inappropriate incentives for managers**
> - There can be accretive bad deals and dilutive good deals.

- the point is that EPS, as a measure, is completely independent of the NPV, thus a deal should not be judged by it

### Accretive(ly) good and bad deals
#### Accretive bad deals
> - Accretive bad deals are those in which the target has a low **P/E** for a good reason.
> 	- because the target is **risky** or operates in a **no-growth** market
> 	- if a high-P/E buyer pays a premium for such a fairly valued target:
> 		- the buyer's EPS rises, creating an apparent growth trend
> 		- the apparent growth is illusory
> 		- the deal destroys value because no premium should have been paid for a target already valued fairly

#### The EPS trap
> - Companies following this strategy become trapped in a vicious cycle:
> 	- to meet the market's EPS-growth expectations, they must keep doing ever-larger deals
> 	- their low-P/E targets are often mediocre, so the portfolio of mediocre firms grows
> 	- eventually the market recognizes the pattern and the acquirer's share price falls
> 	- if managers were dishonest, accounting irregularities and other problems may surface at this point
> 	- examples: Tyco, WorldCom and Carillion

#### Dilutive good deals
> - Dilutive good deals are those in which the target has a **high** P/E for a **good reason**.
> 	- for example, it may be experiencing a temporary crisis of low earnings or operating in a high-growth market
> 	- such acquisitions may dilute the buyer's EPS in the short run but enhance it in the long run
> 	- example:
> 		- a large company uses stock to buy companies with promising growth prospects but low earnings
> 		- large companies keep these acquisitions small so growth in their core business can mask the dilution
> 		- a serial acquirer can therefore complete dilutive good deals while still delivering EPS growth to the market
> 	- Cisco is a well-known example of this strategy

- use of EPS is discussed in this slide

#### Why managers use EPS
> According to Dasgupta, Harford and Ma (2024):
>
> 1. **Synergy cannot be communicated credibly.** Synergies are uncertain, long-term and hard to verify; only about 20% of deals include a management synergy forecast and about 2% disclose an NPV estimate.
> 2. **Combined EPS is objective and verifiable.** Anyone can calculate it from consensus EPS forecasts and the deal terms, without private information or discretion.
> 3. **The habit reinforces itself.** Managers emphasize EPS when it is favourable, priming investors to judge deals that way and encouraging managers to emphasize it again.
>
> Accretion is still not informative about value creation: combined-EPS arithmetic excludes synergies by construction.

#### One target, three ways to pay
> Assume no synergies. Acquirer A has 100 million shares, a $20 share price, EPS of $1, earnings of $100 million and a P/E of 20. Target T has 20 million shares, a $12 share price, EPS of $0.80, earnings of $16 million and a P/E of 15. Cash is financed with debt at 5%, with a 21% tax rate.

| Item | Stock offer: $15.00 | Stock offer: $16.50 | Cash offer: $16.50 |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Premium over $12 market price | 25.0% | 37.5% | 37.5% |
| Multiple paid for T's earnings | 18.8x | 20.6x | 20.6x |
| Compared with A's P/E of 20x | Below | Above | Above |
| Consideration | 0.750 A shares per T share | 0.825 A shares per T share | $330m cash |
| New A shares issued | 15.0m | 16.5m | None |
| Combined earnings | $116.0m | $116.0m | $103.0m |
| Combined shares | 115.0m | 116.5m | 100.0m |
| Combined EPS | $1.009 | $0.996 | $1.030 |
| Change from A's $1.00 EPS | +0.9 cents, accretive | -0.4 cents, dilutive | +3.0 cents, accretive |

> The last two columns offer the same price to the same shareholders; only the payment method changes, yet the EPS verdict flips. Cash earnings are after $13 million of after-tax interest on the $330 million borrowed.

- if you are after an accretive deal, you can adjust the transaction to cash

#### Three lessons from the arithmetic
> 1. **Stock-deal accretion is only a P/E comparison.** A stock deal is accretive if the multiple paid for the target is below the acquirer's own P/E; deal quality does not enter the calculation.
> 2. **Cash removes the denominator effect and costs only interest.** Cash is EPS-friendly when the earnings yield purchased exceeds the after-tax cost of funds. Here, $\frac{16}{330}=4.85\%$ exceeds $5\%\times(1-0.21)=3.95\%$.
> 3. **A small cash component is usually enough.** In mixed deals, the cash share can be tuned until the intended EPS change is just above zero.
>
> Cash is not free: it can cause immediate tax for target shareholders, flotation and interest costs, excess leverage, and cuts to capital expenditure and R&D while the acquirer builds a cash buffer.

- usually combine the stock and cash, adding just enough cash so the deal becomes accretive
- accretive $\implies$ the multiple paid for the target is *below* the acquirer's own P/E
- dilutive $\implies$ the multiple paid for the target is *above* the acquirer's own P/E
- the manipulation can be seen below, so the deal is just about accretive:

> - **Size**
> 	- evidence from 2,299 deals shows that deals which would be barely dilutive if paid entirely in stock use 5-17 percentage points more cash than barely accretive deals
> - **Identification**
> 	- misvaluation, financing costs and merger waves vary smoothly around zero; only the accretive/dilutive label changes discontinuously
> - **Shape**
> 	- cash use declines on both sides: strongly accretive deals need no help, while strongly dilutive deals need more cash than the acquirer can raise

- they use more cash to just become accretive

### Valuing a merger: cash offer
> - Firm A is worth $480 million and firm B is worth $40 million.
> - The merger reduces annual operating costs by $4 million. At a 20% cost of capital, the perpetuity value of the economic gain is:
>   $$\Delta V=\frac{4}{0.20}=\$20\text{ million}$$
> - A offers $19 for each of B's 2.5 million shares, a $3 premium over the current market price.
> - The merger's NPV is:
>   $$NPV=\Delta V-(C-V_B)=20-(2.5\times19-40)=\$12.5\text{ million}$$

- present value of the synergies is 20 mil. using the $PV=\frac{C}{r}$ formula

### Valuing a merger: stock offer
> - Suppose the bidder **offers target shareholders fraction $x$ of the merged firm's shares**.

| Position | Bidder A shareholders | Target B shareholders |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Before merger | $V_A$ | $V_B$ |
| After merger | $(1-x)V_{AB}$ | $xV_{AB}$ |
| Net gain/loss | $(1-x)V_{AB}-V_A$ | $xV_{AB}-V_B$ |

> - The bidder's NPV is:
>   $$NPV=\Delta V-(xV_{AB}-V_B)$$
> - As with a cash offer, the merger's **economic gain** $\Delta V$ is split between the parties through the **cost of acquisition**.

- now dependent on the valuation of the new, merged, company, represented by $x$

#### Example
> - A offers B's shareholders $\frac{1}{3}$ of a share in the merged firm for each B share. A has 10 million shares and B has 2.5 million shares.
> - B's original shareholders receive:
>   $$x=\frac{2.5/3}{10+2.5/3}=7.69\%$$
> - With no cash paid out, the merged firm is worth:
>   $$V_{AB}=480+40+20=\$540\text{ million}$$
> - Therefore:
>   $$NPV=20-(7.69\%\times540-40)=\$18.5\text{ million}$$

- an example of the calculation

### Value effects of M&As
#### Value-increasing theories
> - **Transaction costs**
> 	- firms organize around the appropriate balance between internal operations and external markets (Coase, 1937)
> - **Mergers create synergies** (Bradley, Desai and Kim, 1983, 1988)
> 	- economies of scale
> 	- more effective management
> 	- improved production techniques
> 	- combination of complementary resources
> 	- innovation combination
> - **Takeovers are disciplinary** (Manne, 1965; Alchian and Demsetz, 1972)
> 	- they can remove poor managers
> 	- they facilitate competition between different management teams

#### Value-reducing theories
> - **Agency costs of free cash flow**
> 	- Jensen (1986)
> 	- free cash flow is a source of value-reducing mergers
> 	- firms with free cash flow have internal funds exceeding the investment needed for all positive-NPV projects
> - **Managerial entrenchment**
> 	- Shleifer and Vishny (1989)
> 	- managers are hesitant to distribute cash to shareholders
> 	- they may instead overpay for acquisitions that reduce the likelihood of their own replacement
> - This relates to Masulis, Wang and Xie (2007) on anti-takeover provisions.

- Agency costs of FCF:
	- a *principal-agent problem*:
		- the management and shareholders have separate utility curves
		- there may be a conflict of interest
		- shareholder are the owners/principal, but they bear the consequences of the decisions of the management/agent
		- the management may have different interests than the owners
	- the motivations of the managers:
		- money earning potential which is strongly correlated to managing a larger company
		- thus mergers and especially acquiring become the fastest way to expand, rather than organic growth of a firm 
	- problems arise when the firm runs out of positive NPV project, thus the *only acquisitions left* are the ones that *are value destroying* - Jensen (1986)
- Managerial entrenchment
	- managers do deals that make them very unique to the firm/project thus making themselves irreplaceable, solidifying their position in the firm
- anti-takeover provisions:
	- measures which may be taken by the firm in order to avoid a takeover
	- comparing firms with and without anti-takeover provisions
	- managers are more motivated/efficient in firms lacking anti-takeover provisions, because the threat of a takeover is always looming, where as in a firm with anti-takeover provisions, the managers may not be as efficient, because their position is more secure
	- firms with anti-takeover provisions had much smaller market reaction to being acquired

#### Value-neutral theory: hubris
> - Merger bids may result from managerial hubris: managers are prone to **excessive self-confidence** (Roll, 1986).
> - CEO hubris can explain why bids are made even when a valuation above the current market price is a positive valuation error.
> - If synergy is zero, **$\Delta V=0$**, bidders should not pay a premium for the target.
> - Hubris explains why some managers nevertheless pay a premium and destroy shareholder value.

#### Hubris and observed bids
> - The situation:
> 	- the target has an observable market price
> 	- if we **assume no synergies** and valuation is a random variable, offers below the market price are not observed
> 	- an offer is made only when the bidder's valuation is above the current target price
> 	- the observed takeover premium may therefore be a selected positive valuation error

- we are looking at a publicly listed firm, thus we have an observable price of the target
- only bids above the current price are observed (the ones below do not matter)

> - If takeovers create no value, **$\Delta V=0$**, why do firms bid and pay a premium?
> 	- CEOs should recognize that any bid above market price represents an error
> - Takeovers are infrequent individual decisions and are prone to individual irrationality.
> 	- CEOs may convince themselves that their valuation is right and the market does not reflect the full value of the combined firm: **managerial hubris**
> 	- the market can remain rational in aggregate even when individual decision-makers are not
> - Even when some mergers create genuine gains, part of the average observed premium may still be caused by hubris.
> - Because valuations are uncertain, bidders should allow for estimation error as they would for the winner's curse.

- even though the bids should reflect the market price + premium, it is argued that some part of the premium paid is managerial hubris
- overall, market is rational, but individual offers may not be

#### Winner's curse
> - In auctions:
> 	- oil companies **bidding for offshore drilling rights** in first-price auctions sometimes paid more than the contracts proved to be worth
> 	- oil companies are nevertheless good at estimating oil reserves on average
> 	- in another example, the winner **bidding for a jar of coins** pays 25% more than its value on average
> 	- these are auctions for objects with an uncertain intrinsic value: a common-value setting
> - Competitive bidding produces a distribution of value estimates.
> - The manager with the most optimistic forecast wins the bidding process.
> - The winner is cursed because the winning bid likely overvalues the target.
> - A safer strategy is to discount the bid, with the discount also depending on the number of bidders.

- this is highlighting the winner's problem in an FPA
- the strategy is to bid lower than their real bid to get around the winner's curse

### Patterns of gains

| Theory                    | Combined gains | Gains to target | Gains to bidder |
| ------------------------- | -------------: | --------------: | --------------: |
| Efficiency/synergy        |       Positive |        Positive |    Non-negative |
| Agency costs/entrenchment |       Negative |        Positive |   More negative |
| Hubris                    |           Zero |        Positive |        Negative |

- Efficiency/Synergy:
	- targets typically get a 30 - 40% premium, thus the target gains are usually positive
	- gains to bidder should be non-negative
	- very large number of acquirerers have negative gains after an acquisition
- Agency Costs/Entrenchment:
	- already there is a negative synergy, and by the additional costs associated with a merger, the bidder loses even more value
- Hubris:
	- value destroying acquisitions

### Post-merger restructuring
> - Maksimovic, Phillips and Prabhala (2011) study acquirers' restructuring for up to three years after acquisitions.
> - M&As let firms grow quickly and reconfigure their asset portfolios, after which acquirers must decide **how to redraw firm boundaries**.
> - The paper shows that:
> 	- acquirers **do not passively retain** acquired assets
> 	- the merger starts a **vigorous restructuring**: within three years they sell or close 46% of the plants they bought, more than matched firms or assets in partial-firm acquisitions

#### Which assets are kept, sold or closed?
> - The results are consistent with acquirers **using their comparative advantage** across industries to restructure targets.
> 	- they are more likely to **retain** acquired plants when they already **operate a plant in the same industry**, especially when the plants add to **their largest divisions**
> 	- plants in **peripheral target divisions** are more likely to be **sold**, particularly when market prices for those assets are high
> 	- even when buying an entire firm, acquirers may therefore be interested ex ante in only a subset of its assets

#### Performance of transferred plants
> - Performance of plants transferred in acquisitions:
> 	- acquired plants **kept** by the acquirer **tend to improve in performance**, with significant increases in productivity and operating margins
> 	- performance of sold plants tends to remain flat
> 	- this holds for whole-firm mergers followed by resales and for the more limited sales following partial-firm acquisitions
> 	- improvement in retained plants is related to how well the acquirer runs its existing businesses
> - Overall, the results do not support agency-based theories of M&As.

- the target finally decides

### The private takeover process
> - **The sale process** usually starts with either:
> 	- a prospective buyer approaching a target, or
> 	- management deciding to offer its company for sale
> 	- this is called **deal initiation**
> - The selling company's management and financial adviser arrange an **auction** or **negotiate** privately with an exclusive buyer (Hansen, 2001).
> 	- private negotiation involves one interested bidder, such as Wrigley's sale to Mars in 2008
> 	- auctions involve competition: the seller may choose a **full-scale auction** or a **controlled sale**

### Formal auctions
#### Setup and information
> - A formal auction is more structured.
> 	- the sale process is predetermined by the target and its financial adviser and follows **multiple planned rounds**
> - The financial adviser:
> 	- serves as the auctioneer
> 	- prepares a list of **potential bidders** and contacts them to determine their interest
> - Interested parties:
> 	- receive a cursory description of the seller
> 	- receive a detailed information memorandum after signing a **confidentiality agreement**

- the process happens in stages, more and more information is given to the potential bidders in each successive stage
- the further information is only distributed after the confidentiality agreement has been signed

#### Preliminary offers and due diligence
> - Bidders submit **preliminary, non-binding offers**.
> 	- these are letters of intent
> 	- bidders provide an indicative valuation range for the target
> 	- the auctioneer uses these indications to **reduce the number of bidders** proceeding further
> - In the next round:
> 	- remaining bidders receive **access to a data room**
> 	- visits and senior-management presentations are arranged for deeper investigation
> - **Restricting information to bidders** contradicts standard auction theory.
> 	- the concern is the **value of that information** to the seller's competitors, suppliers and customers

#### Final bids
> - In the final step:
> 	- bidders submit sealed **binding bids**
> 	- the seller chooses the **best bid** based on valuation, financing structure and the bidder's future plans
> 	- usually one buyer is selected
> 	- the buyer receives an **exclusivity period** to resolve remaining issues and complete **final due diligence** before executing a definitive acquisition agreement

### Controlled sales
> - A full-scale auction is not the only form of **competitive bidding**.
> - In a **controlled sale**, the seller negotiates with a limited number of bidders.
> - Example: Autonomy's takeover of Virage.
> 	- buyer-initiated sale in March 2003
> 	- Virage negotiated with three bidders and received initial offers from all three
> 	- the board selected Autonomy's offer and signed an exclusivity agreement on 3 June 2003
> 	- due diligence was conducted
> 	- the merger agreement was executed and publicly announced on 10 July 2003

- something between an auction and a 1:1 negotiation, as described below
- didn't organize the whole sale process, but there were multiple bidders competing

### Private selling process
> - The activity before a deal announcement is **hidden from the public eye**.
> - It reveals a robust **competitive environment**.
> - Studying only the public process and publicly observed competition gives a biased picture.

### Some statistics

> - Of 400 firms, 202 were sold in an auction.
> - In an average auction:
> 	- 21 bidders were contacted
> 	- seven signed confidentiality agreements
> 	- 1.7 submitted a written bid
> 	- 1.2 publicly announced a formal bid
> - Targets sold in auctions and their bidders were smaller, but relative target-to-bidder size was not different.

### What is the best way to sell a company?
> - Common sense suggests competition is beneficial.
> 	- more competing bids should produce higher prices
> - Evidence instead suggests **one size does not fit all**.
> 	- auctions may produce a higher premium for some firms, while private negotiation may be equally effective for others (Boone and Mulherin, 2009)
> 	- premiums in private negotiations are at least as high as those in auctions (Boone and Mulherin, 2007)

### Auctions in theoretical models
> - The contest producing the winning offer is commonly modelled as an **English auction**.
> - In a simple auction model:
> 	- bidders make offers and counteroffers at **no cost**
> 	- each offer exceeds the previous one until the highest-valuation bidder wins
> 	- the winner pays the second-highest bidder's valuation
> 	- the auction produces an **efficient allocation of control**
> 	- the second-price principle permits truthful revelation of valuations

### Costs of bidding
#### Bidder's trade-off
> - In reality, bidders incur:
> 	- **search costs** to find potential targets
> 	- **valuation costs** to learn their private target valuation or revise their bids
> - Trade-off for the **bidder**:
> 	- greater competition reduces the probability of success
> 	- when evaluation costs and competition are high, a bidder may offer a lower premium or decline to participate

#### Seller's trade-off
> - Trade-off for the **seller (target)**:
> 	- search and evaluation costs may make limited participation induce more **aggressive bidding**, because invited bidders have a higher probability of winning and a higher expected value from bidding
> 	- sellers may also limit competition to prevent **information leakage** to competitors
> - Auction costs can substantially limit their apparent benefits (Boone and Mulherin, 2007).

### Pre-emptive bidding
> - Pre-emptive bidding explains why private negotiations may produce high premiums (Fishman, 1988).
> 	- bidders must pay **an investigation/evaluation cost** to learn their private target valuations
> 	- if both bidders enter **at the same time**:
> 		- both investigation costs are sunk
> 		- a costless English auction follows and produces the efficient outcome
> 	- if one bidder offers first:
> 		- a sufficiently high initial bid **deters** the second bidder from paying the investigation cost and entering
> 		- the high bid signals a high private valuation, reducing rivals' expected probability of winning
> 		- if the investigation cost is high enough, a rival's expected value becomes negative and it does not enter

- the strategy is bidding high to deter the other bidders, as they have yet to pay the valuation cost
- if my bid is high, then the expected win for the next bidder is smaller

### Complementarities and total synergy
> - Based on Fidrmuc, Koufopoulos and Xia (2021).
> - The target firm:
> 	- chooses the selling method
> 	- usually understands its competitive environment
> 	- can identify bidders with highly complementary assets and the highest synergies
> - Providing information costs **$c$ per bidder**.
> - The optimal strategy is to contact, inform and invite only bidders with complementary assets and high synergy.
> - The model predicts a negative relationship between complementarity and the number of bidders.
> - Higher synergies permit a larger target premium, controlling for the target's bargaining power.

- when the seller knows the distribution of the offers of the bidders, then it is worth it to invite only the ones with the highest valuations
- looking at lower number of bidders, with higher premiums

### Merger arbitrage
> - Merger arbitrage is speculation on the outcome of a takeover bid.
> 	- it occurs after the deal announcement
> 	- trading before announcement using private information is illegal
> 	- it is a bet on the probability that the deal completes
> 	- it is also called risk arbitrage, although that term is broader

#### Arbitrage spread
> - Merger arbitrage exploits the arbitrage spread.
> 	- the spread is the difference between the acquisition price and the target's trading price before completion
> 	- it is realized between announcement and completion, an average of 105 days in the cited data
> 	- the discount reflects a completion probability below one
> 	- the strategy bears the risk of failure, so it is not truly risk-free arbitrage
> 	- nevertheless, merger arbitrage is associated with large excess returns and is a common hedge-fund strategy

- if the market believes, that the offer is low and that the probability of the merger going through is high, the market will react in such a way that the stock price at the time of announcement will be higher than the offer price
- the arbitrage spread is also used as a predictor of the probability of the deal closing according to the market
- the jump above the offer price will be immediate 
- the spread will decrease overtime, as more information becomes publically available

## Lecture 3

### What happens to stock prices around M&As?
> - Using financial-market data, an **event study** measures the effect of a specific event on firm value.
> 	- it relies on the **semi-strong form** of market efficiency (Fama, 1970)
> 	- **new public information** is reflected in share prices immediately
> 	- this lets us measure the event's **economic impact**
> 	- the impact can be measured over a relatively short period

### Event study methodology
> - The first papers introducing the method were:
> 	- Ball and Brown (1968) on earnings
> 	- Fama, Fisher, Jensen and Roll (1969) on stock splits
> - It has since become an established methodology.
> - It changed how researchers view stock-market responses to information.
> - In **chronological time**, the reaction appears chaotic and patternless.
> - In **event time**, a distinctive pattern becomes visible.

### The main idea
> - Test how an **information release** affects share prices.
> 	- the information must be **new** to the public domain
> 	- its release date must be known
> 	- for an M&A, use **the first public announcement**
> 	- define the announcement as the **event day**, $t=0$
> 	- rearrange all observations relative to that day
> - Null hypothesis: the event has no effect on the distribution of returns.

### The procedure
> - The timeline consists of:
> 	- estimation window: $T_0$ to $T_1$
> 	- event window: $T_1$ to $T_2$, containing $t=0$
> 	- post-event window: $T_2$ to $T_3$
> - Event windows in M&A studies are often $(-5,+5)$.

- we can see that this is very similar to the arbitrage mode of visualization, except more granular

### Abnormal returns
> - The objective is to measure the **economic impact** of an event on firm value.
> - The realized return is **$R_{i,t}$**:
> 	- for firm **$i$** on date **$t$**
> - The normal return is the return that would have occurred without the event.
> - It is the expected return without conditioning on the event, **$E(R_{i,t})$**.
> - The **abnormal return** is the return in excess of the expected return:
>   $$\boldsymbol{AR_{i,t}=R_{i,t}-E(R_{i,t})}$$

### Modelling the normal return
> - Expected normal returns cannot be observed directly and must be estimated.
> - A model must be chosen to predict returns.
> - A simple assumption is that the firm's return would equal the market's average return that day.
> - Normal return on day **$t$** in the event window:
>   $$\boldsymbol{E(R_{i,t})=R_{M,t}}$$
> - Abnormal return for event **$i$**:
>   $$\boldsymbol{AR_{i,t}=R_{i,t}-R_{M,t}}$$
> - This is the **market-adjusted model**.

- the abnormal return is the difference between the realized return and the normal return on that day
- measures if the announcement made the stock/market move that it would have if there were no announcement, or if it were just noise, or otherwise normal market movement

### Aggregation of abnormal returns
> - So far, abnormal returns **$AR_{i,t}$** have been calculated for each event **$i$** on day **$t$**.
> - Aggregate the effects to estimate the event's impact **on average**.
> - Average abnormal returns over all **$N$** events:
>   $$\boldsymbol{AAR_t=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}AR_{i,t}}$$
> 	- **$AAR_t$** is the average abnormal return across all events on date **$t$**

### Testing
> - Test whether the event affects firm value.
> 	- the average abnormal return differs from zero
> 	- this difference must be tested for **statistical significance**
> - **Null hypothesis:** on average, the event has no effect on returns:
>   $$\boldsymbol{H_0:AAR_t=0}$$
> - The most common test uses a t-statistic:
>   $$\boldsymbol{\frac{AAR_t}{\sigma/\sqrt{N}}\sim N(0,1)}$$
> 	- assume IID normally distributed returns with zero mean and variance $\sigma^2$; $N$ is the number of events
> 	- estimate $\sigma$ using the cross-sectional variance of abnormal returns:
>   $$\boldsymbol{s_t^2=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(AR_{i,t}-AAR_t)^2}$$
>   $$\boldsymbol{\frac{AAR_t}{s_t/\sqrt{N}}\sim t_{N-1}}$$

### Cumulation of abnormal returns
> - Event windows are often wider than one day.
> 	- for example, an 11-day window around the event: $(-5,+5)$
> - Aggregate abnormal returns for firm **$i$** over the window **$[\tau_1,\tau_2]$**.
> 	- cumulative abnormal return:
>   $$\boldsymbol{CAR_{i,\tau_1,\tau_2}=\sum_{t=\tau_1}^{\tau_2}AR_{i,t}}$$
> 	- buy-and-hold abnormal return:
>   $$\boldsymbol{BHAR_{i,\tau_1,\tau_2}=\prod_{t=\tau_1}^{\tau_2}(1+R_{i,t})-1-\left[\prod_{t=\tau_1}^{\tau_2}(1+E(R_{i,t}))-1\right]}$$
> - Buy-and-hold abnormal returns are used to measure long-term effects.
> - Average the results across events and test their significance.
> 	- the variance of cumulative abnormal returns can again be estimated using cross-sectional variance

### Nonparametric tests
> - The preceding methods are parametric.
> 	- they require assumptions about the distribution of abnormal returns
> - Nonparametric tests, such as sign and rank tests, do not require distributional assumptions.
> - Sign test:
> 	- uses whether abnormal returns are positive or negative
> 	- requires independence across securities; under the null, 50% of abnormal returns are expected to be positive
>   $$\boldsymbol{\theta=\frac{N^+-0.5N}{0.5\sqrt{N}}\sim N(0,1)}$$
> 	- **$N^+$** is the number of positive abnormal returns
> 	- **$N$** is the total number of abnormal returns

### Other benchmarks: the market model
> - The most common model for normal returns is the **market model**.
> - It assumes a stable linear relationship between the market return and the security return.
> - The expected-return process is:
>   $$\boldsymbol{E(R_{i,t+1})=\alpha_i+\beta_iE(R_{M,t+1})}$$

### Market model
> - For each event **$i$**, estimate the normal return by estimating **$\alpha_i$** and **$\beta_i$** over the estimation window:
>   $$\boldsymbol{R_{i,t}=\alpha_i+\beta_iR_{M,t}+\varepsilon_{i,t}}$$
> - This produces estimates **$\hat{\alpha}_i$** and **$\hat{\beta}_i$**.
> - Normal return on day **$t$** in the event window:
>   $$\boldsymbol{E(R_{i,t})=\hat{\alpha}_i+\hat{\beta}_iR_{M,t}}$$
> - Abnormal return for event **$i$**:
>   $$\boldsymbol{AR_{i,t}=R_{i,t}-(\hat{\alpha}_i+\hat{\beta}_iR_{M,t})}$$

- we are making it more flexible, estimating $\alpha$ and $\beta$ for each individual firm
- we are running a specific market model for each of them

### The procedure
> - Estimation window: at least 180 days, from $T_0$ to $T_1$.
> - Event window: $(-5,+5)$ in this example, from $T_1$ to $T_2$ and containing $t=0$.
> - Post-event window: $T_2$ to $T_3$.
> - Estimation and event windows usually do not overlap.

- we need separate data for each of the firms, often 180 daily observations
- run the regression, and then run the expected return $\mathbb{E}(R_{i,t})$ using firm-specific data 

### Market-adjusted model
> - A simplified version of the market model.
> - Assume **$\alpha=0$** and **$\beta=1$**.
> - No estimation is required, making it suitable when pre-event returns are unavailable, such as for IPOs.

- for example, for first just after IPO, we cannot use the market model, because we do not have the data before the IPO

### Constant mean return model
> - Assumes returns fluctuate around a long-term mean:
>   $$\boldsymbol{R_{i,t}=\mu_i+\zeta_{i,t}}$$
> 	- **$\mu_i$** is firm $i$'s mean return and **$\zeta_{i,t}$** is the deviation from that mean on day **$t$**
>   $$\boldsymbol{E(\zeta_{i,t})=0,\qquad \operatorname{var}(\zeta_{i,t})=\sigma_{\zeta_i}^2}$$
> - Estimate each firm's mean return **$\hat{\mu}_i$** over the estimation window.
> - Estimated normal return:
>   $$\boldsymbol{E(R_{i,t})=\hat{\mu}_i}$$
> - Estimated abnormal return:
>   $$\boldsymbol{AR_{i,t}=R_{i,t}-\hat{\mu}_i}$$

- a simplified case of the market model above

### Factor models
#### Industry-adjusted model
> - A factor model is a general statistical model.
> - Factor models can reduce abnormal-return variance by explaining more variation in normal returns.
> - Factors are typically **portfolios of traded securities**.
> - The market model is a one-factor model.
> - Industry-adjusted model:
> 	- partitions listed firms into industries
> 	- models firm **$i$**'s normal return at time **$t$** using the return **$R_{j,t}$** for industry **$j$**
> 	- abnormal return:
>   $$\boldsymbol{AR_{i,j,t}=R_{i,t}-R_{j,t}}$$

- the market model is a simplified version of the factor model

#### Size-adjusted model
> - Partitions listed firms into market-capitalization groups, usually deciles.
> - Firm **$i$**'s normal return at time **$t$** is the return **$R_{k,t}$** for its corresponding size decile **$k$**.
> - Abnormal return:
>   $$\boldsymbol{AR_{i,k,t}=R_{i,t}-R_{k,t}}$$

- can take different variables as groups (like size, market/book ratio, ...)

#### Fama-French three-factor model
> - The return-generating process uses three factors:
>   $$\boldsymbol{R_{i,t}=\alpha_i+\beta_iR_{M,t}+s_iSMB_t+h_iHML_t+\varepsilon_{i,t}}$$
> - **$SMB_t$** is the average return on small-cap portfolios minus the average return on large-cap portfolios.
> - **$HML_t$** is the average return on high book-to-market portfolios minus the average return on low book-to-market portfolios.
> - The three factors are market, size and value.
> - Abnormal return:
>   $$\boldsymbol{AR_{i,t}=R_{i,t}-(\hat{\alpha}_i+\hat{\beta}_iR_{M,t}+\hat{s}_iSMB_t+\hat{h}_iHML_t)}$$

- factors easily available online
- estimate firm-specific $\hat{\alpha}, \hat{\beta}, \hat{s}_{i}, \hat{h}_{i}$

#### Four-factor model
> - Adds momentum:
> 	- **$UMD_t$**, up minus down
> 	- based on returns over the previous year
> 	- the average return on two high-prior-return portfolios minus the average return on two low-prior-return portfolios
> - Multifactor models generally offer limited gains for event studies.
> 	- additional factors have little marginal explanatory power, so they reduce abnormal-return variance only slightly

### Critique of CARs in M&As
#### Ben-David et al. (2026)
> - CARs do not correlate with common or newer ex-post outcome measures:
> 	- goodwill impairments
> 	- abnormal profitability
> 	- deal completion
> - A simple model using information known at announcement predicts these outcomes reasonably well, but CARs do not even capture that model's prediction.
> 	- four common characteristics: payment form, target public status, acquirer size and relative deal size
> 	- value is **destroyed** in cash-only deals, public-target deals, deals by non-large acquirers and deals large relative to the acquirer; value is created in the mirror-image cases
> - Source: Ben-David, Bhattacharya, Huang and Jacobsen (2026), “The (missing) relation between acquisition announcement returns and value creation,” *Journal of Finance* 81, 1265-1320.

- after a firm realizes that the deal they had done was not as good as they had hoped $\implies$ put it into 'goodwill impairments' 
	- this becomes a good benchmark of how the deal went
- CARs does **not** measure value created in deals
	- lower CARs should have lower goodwill impairments
	- lower CARs should have higher abnormal returns
	- lower CARs should have higher deal completion
- they are, however, not good at prediction of the outcomes
- if we use the most common characteristics, the prediction is reasonably good, CARs are not
- perhaps (short-term) CARs should not be used as predictors of value creation
	- arguably not about market efficiency
	- implies that the market is looking at other things

#### Standalone acquirer information
> - Information about the standalone acquirer dominates CARs, making deal-related information difficult to extract.
> 	- overvaluation
> 	- strategy
> 	- managerial skill
> 	- managerial risk aversion
> 	- investment opportunities

- doesn't communicate about the deal, but about the acquirerer as a standalone company
	- reacts to the variables mentioned above

### M&A decisions

> - A key issue is whether an acquisition creates value for **acquiring** shareholders.

### Method of payment

#### Tax explanation

> - Cash and stock payments receive different tax treatment in the United States:
> 	- **all-cash** acquisitions trigger immediate capital-gains taxation
> 	- **all-stock** acquisitions defer capital-gains taxation until the received shares are sold
> 	- the treatment of **mixed cash-stock** acquisitions depends on the cash portion and other transaction characteristics
> - Target shareholders may demand a higher premium to **compensate** for the differential tax burden.
> - The compensation depends on the parties' **relative bargaining power**.
> - The effect is **transaction specific**, making consistent cross-sectional evidence difficult to obtain.

- tax:
- tax is not very useful in cross-sectional studies, due to lack of information
- time-value of money difference between cash and stock
	- present value of tax is higher, due to cash
	- sometimes, if in a good negotiating position, the seller will negotiate a higher cash price, to be compensated for the increased ammount of tax they have to pay

#### Asymmetric-information models

> - The bidder and target can each be asymmetrically informed about the true value of their own shares.
> - The choice of payment causes **information revelation**, which affects:
> 	- the **division of synergy gains**
> 	- the **probability that the offer is successful**
> - Several models examine these effects.

- asymmetric information:
- the firm management has the bulk of the information, whereas the shareholder has less
- if, as a target, you reveal what payment you prefer, you reveal information which may help the bidders make their bid higher or lower

#### Information asymmetry—the bidder's value

> - Target shareholders and the market do not have full information about the value of the **bidder's shares** (Myers and Majluf).
> - **Adverse selection** arises when bidder stock is overpriced:
> 	- targets resist stock offers
> 	- an all-stock offer produces a negative market reaction
> - Evidence:
> 	- bidder abnormal returns are negative for all-stock acquisitions of public targets
> 	- bidder abnormal returns are positive for all-stock acquisitions of private targets
> 	- after suitable adjustments, Golubov et al. find that stock acquisitions are not value destructive

- model with only low and high value firm, where we are willing to pay the average price, $\hat{p}$ , explain what the different firms would prefer and what the offer signals:
	- if a firm offers stock payment, it signals that it is overvalued
	- and so on

- corporate control within the bidder firm
- behavioural arguments
- if we have a model with low and high price bidders, and the distribution, we can calculate the expected price (in terms of the stock price)
	- when approaching this bidder, the seller does not know, therefore the information asymetry
	- does not apply to cash obviously (the value is self-evident)
	- when we get stock, we are interested in this - we get shares either in low or in high type (if we pay the average for a low value stock, we overpay), and perhaps the high-value stock bidder won't offer stock, because it would indicate that they are overpriced
- thus the form of the payment offer itself is a hint as to the valuation of the bidder
- an adverse selection issue

#### Information asymmetry—the target's value: case 1

> - The key insight from Hansen (1987) is that stock payment has an ex-post effect on target shareholders.
> - Because target shareholders are paid in bidder shares, they **bear the value consequences** of changes in the bidder's price and **share in the appreciation or depreciation of the merged firm**.
> - Model:
> 	- bidder $B$ is uncertain about the target's value
> 	- the bidder is willing to pay **$v$**, including the target's standalone value and synergies
> 	- claim: if **$v_H>v$**, target shareholders are better off with stock payment
> 	- consider only all-stock and all-cash offers

- this time the bidder doesn't know
- claim: shareholders in the firm that know their firm of the high type prefer the stock compensation

##### Cash versus stock

> - With a **cash offer**, the bidder offers **$v$**.
> - With a **stock offer**, the bidder offers an ownership fraction **$z$**.
> - Set $z$ so that the offer has value $v$:
> 	- **$v=z(v+v_B)$**
> 	- **$z=\dfrac{v}{v+v_B}$**
> - If the target is high-value, **$v_H>v$**, stock is better for its shareholders:
> 	- **$z(v_H+v_B)=\dfrac{v}{v+v_B}(v_H+v_B)=v\dfrac{v_H+v_B}{v+v_B}>v$**
> - Stock payment **extracts** a higher value because the informed target shareholders share in the merged firm's higher value.

- $z$ is set up such that the value of it is equal to the value their willing to pay for it
- they get fraction $z$ but they know their value is high
- in cash offer they get $v$
- in stock offer they get the $v$ something, which is higher
- intuition is that you know the value is higher so you want to obtain the higher value
	- also that since you already are a shareholder, the higher obtained value is directly translated

##### Acceptance decision

> - Stock payment can produce a **more efficient accept/reject decision**: an overpriced target may accept a stock offer while rejecting the equivalent cash offer.
> - Let **$v$** include the target's standalone value and the acquisition synergies.
> - For a listed target, the observable standalone market price is **$p$**, so the target will not consider an offer with **$v<p$**.
> - If the target's fundamental value is **$f$**, value-increasing takeovers can fail when **$f<v<p$**.
> - A stock bid may carry a sufficiently high value to be accepted.

- cash deal would get rejected because it would be just below the market price, whereas they would accept a stock bid

#### Information asymmetry—the target's value: case 2

> - All-stock payment may **increase the bidder's expected deal value** when the target's value is uncertain but the bidder's value is known.
> - For bidder **$B$**, the combined firm's value is **$v+v_B$**.
> - The bidder knows that the target value is distributed over **$v\in[\underline v,\bar v]$**.
> - If the bidder's strategy is to **ensure bid success**, a cash bid must be **$c=\bar v$**.
> - Expected cash overpayment is **$\bar v-E(v\mid\mathrm{accept})$**.

- $v+v_{B}$ is the value of the newly created firm

##### Why the bidder prefers stock

> - With stock payment, the bidder pays using shares in the merged firm.
> - To offer the highest possible target value:
> 	- **$\bar v=z(\bar v+v_B)$**
> 	- **$z=\dfrac{\bar v}{\bar v+v_B}$**
> - **Expected overpayment** with stock is:
> 	- **$z[E(v\mid\mathrm{accept})+v_B]-E(v\mid\mathrm{accept})=\dfrac{v_B}{\bar v+v_B}[\bar v-E(v\mid\mathrm{accept})]$**
> - Because **$\dfrac{v_B}{\bar v+v_B}<1$**, expected overpayment is lower than with cash.
> - The target **shares the ex-post overpayment**, whereas cash precommits the bidder to the full amount.

#### Trade-offs and two-sided asymmetry

> - The **method of payment affects the bidder's costs**:
> 	- stock reduces the cost of overpayment because the target shares it
> 	- cash makes the bidder bear the entire overpayment
> 	- stock increases the probability of success
> 	- failure with cash costs the bidder the forgone synergy gains
> - With two-sided information asymmetry, **cash is more costly** and can therefore credibly signal a high-quality bidder.

- signalling:
	- if the target doesn't know the bidder type, it can signal it (some statement for example)
	- has to be credible, otherwise the lower type can just copy it 
	- comes from the party with the information
	- in this sense, the signal has to be costly; but it is costlier for the lower type than the higher type, thus making it credible
- the method of payment is used as a strategic tool
- final intuition is that cash is more costly, so it can be used to signal

Semi-strong market efficiency motivates using a short window: new public information should be reflected promptly in prices. Set each announcement to event day zero so different calendar dates can be compared. 	**AR, AAR, CAR, CAAR and BHAR — what each measures**

	Let $R_{i,t}$ be the actual return for firm/event $i$ on day $t$, and $\widehat R^{normal}_{i,t}$ its estimated normal return for that same day.

	- **AR — abnormal return: one firm/event, one day.** $AR_{i,t}=R_{i,t}-\widehat R^{normal}_{i,t}$. It asks: “How much better or worse did this stock perform than its benchmark today?” Actual return of 3% versus expected return of 1% gives AR of **+2 percentage points**. Actual return of −1% versus expected return of −3% also gives AR of +2 points: abnormal performance can be positive even when the stock price falls. AR is not necessarily positive and is not the raw return.
	- **AAR — average abnormal return: many events, the same relative day.** $AAR_t=\frac{1}{N}\sum_i AR_{i,t}$. Align all announcements at day zero, then average their day-zero ARs, for example. It asks: “What is the typical abnormal response on this day relative to announcement?” The sample usually contains different firms' deals; it need not be one firm's repeated acquisitions. Averaging helps reduce event-specific noise.
	- **CAR — cumulative abnormal return: one event, several days, added.** $CAR_{i,[a,b]}=\sum_{t=a}^{b}AR_{i,t}$. It asks: “What is the sum of abnormal daily performance across the event window?” A wider window can capture information arriving before the official announcement or adjustment after it. CAR adds daily deviations; it is **not** a compounded investment return.
	- **CAAR — cumulative average abnormal return: many events, several days.** $CAAR_{[a,b]}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_i CAR_{i,[a,b]}$. For the same equally weighted sample on every day, this also equals $\sum_{t=a}^{b}AAR_t$. It is the average CAR across events. This label makes explicit the distinction between averaging across deals and accumulating across days.
	- **BHAR — buy-and-hold abnormal return: one investment horizon, compounded.** $BHAR_{i,[a,b]}=\prod_{t=a}^{b}(1+R_{i,t})-\prod_{t=a}^{b}(1+\widehat R^{normal}_{i,t})$. It asks: “How much more or less would a buy-and-hold investment earn than buying and holding the benchmark over the same period?” Compound the actual returns and benchmark returns **separately**, then subtract. Do **not** compound ARs. BHAR is useful for longer horizons because gains and losses change the amount invested for subsequent periods; individual BHARs can then be averaged across firms.

	**Small illustration to explain the difference, not an exam calculation:** suppose actual returns are +10% on each of two days and benchmark returns are +5% on each day. Each day's AR is +5 percentage points, so CAR is +10 points. Actual buy-and-hold return is 21%, while the benchmark earns 10.25%, so BHAR is +10.75 points. The difference comes from compounding. CAR and BHAR are often close over short windows with small returns, but need not be close over long horizons.
		
	**Where the benchmark comes from.** The **market model** estimates firm-specific $\alpha$ and $\beta$ from pre-event returns, then uses $\hat\alpha_i+\hat\beta_iR_{M,t}$ to predict the firm's normal return on each event-window day. This allows firms to have different market sensitivities. The **market-adjusted model** simply uses that day's market return, effectively setting $\alpha=0$ and $\beta=1$, so it needs no parameter-estimation window. A **constant-mean model** uses the firm's estimated historical mean instead. “Normal” is model-dependent; it does not mean a guaranteed return.

	**Remember:** AR = one daily deviation; AAR = average across events; CAR = add across days; CAAR = average those CARs; BHAR = compound actual and benchmark paths separately, then compare.

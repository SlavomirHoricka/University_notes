The introductory question is how economics can explain decision-making more accurately when psychology matters. Behavioral economics changes specific assumptions about motivations, self-control, attention, beliefs, or the stability of preferences. It retains economic models and the requirement that their predictions can be tested. The introduction therefore provides a framework for later models, without yet teaching their detailed equations or research findings. [[00_Materials/2026_2027/Winter_Semester/Behavioral_Economics/Week_1/L1 BE_Introduction IES 2026 v2.pdf#page=2|Introduction, PDF slides 2–3]]

Experimental economics concerns how evidence is gathered. Researchers can control information, incentives, and context to test predictions and move closer to estimating causal effects. Naturally observed economic processes have the advantage of remaining undisturbed, but associations between variables can be difficult to interpret causally. Behavioral economics and experimental economics thus have a close connection while remaining distinct: one supplies explanations about choices, and the other supplies an empirical method frequently used to test them. [[00_Materials/2026_2027/Winter_Semester/Behavioral_Economics/Week_1/L1 BE_Introduction IES 2026 v2.pdf#page=5|Introduction, PDF slides 5–6]]

The course builds lectures around theory or intuition, evidence mainly from controlled experiments, and applications. Microeconomics I provides the prerequisite framework. Week 1 covers the definition, the method distinction, and course requirements; the syllabus's later topics and the final-exam sample questions are recorded as orientation without adding later-week teaching content. ### Social preferences in the expanded Week 1 collection

The additional Social Preferences presentation makes the introduction concrete. In the ultimatum game a receiver can reject, while in the dictator game the split is implemented without a veto. The reported giving and rejection patterns motivate models in which utility includes another person's payoff, unequal outcomes, or kind and unkind treatment. The model uses a positive or negative other-payoff weight that can change when a person moves from being ahead to being behind; reciprocity instead responds to treatment. [[00_Materials/2026_2027/Winter_Semester/Behavioral_Economics/Week_1/L2 BE_SocPrefs IES 2026.pdf#page=3|Social Preferences, physical PDF pp. 3, 8–22]]

Trust and the prisoner's dilemma extend the question from distributing a fixed pie to creating surplus. In the trust game, sending money increases the joint monetary payoff but leaves the investor dependent on the trustee's return. In the prisoner's dilemma, mutual cooperation pays both players more than mutual defection even though each selfish player has a private incentive to defect. The new notes derive the selfish benchmarks, distinguish empirical transfers from a uniquely identified motive, and state when warm-glow or reciprocity can alter choices. They preserve limitations in the country table and reported return percentage rather than infer missing study details.

# Introduction to Econometrics

## Definition and Scope

**Econometrics** is the quantitative application of statistical and mathematical methods to economic data for the purpose of testing economic theories, estimating relationships among economic variables, and forecasting economic quantities.

Econometrics occupies the intersection of:
- **Economic theory** — supplies the theoretical model specifying which variables are related and in what direction,
- **Mathematical statistics** — provides tools for estimation and inference,
- **Data** — empirical observations that allow the model to be estimated.

The cardinal goal is to interpret causal relationships, not merely correlations. This is notoriously difficult with observational (non-experimental) data.

### Why Econometrics Differs from Pure Statistics

Unlike classical statistics, which is often concerned with controlled experiments, econometrics predominantly deals with **observational data** generated by economic processes. This introduces challenges:
- **Endogeneity**: explanatory variables may be correlated with the error term,
- **Omitted variable bias**: relevant determinants of $y$ may be unobserved,
- **Simultaneity / reverse causation**: $x$ may affect $y$ and $y$ may affect $x$ simultaneously.

## Data Structures in Econometrics

Econometric models are estimated on three canonical data structures:

### 1. Cross-Sectional Data
A sample of **individuals, firms, cities, or countries** observed at a *single point in time*.

- Observations: $i = 1, 2, \ldots, n$ (independent units)
- Key assumption: **random sampling** — each unit is drawn independently from the population.
- Standard regression tools (OLS) apply directly under random sampling.

### 2. Time Series Data
A sequence of observations on **one unit** (e.g., GDP of a country) recorded over $T$ time periods: $t = 1, 2, \ldots, T$.

- Observations are ordered in time and typically **serially correlated** (today's error correlates with yesterday's error).
- Temporal dependence violates the standard i.i.d. assumption.
- Requires special tools: ARIMA, VAR models, cointegration.

### 3. Panel Data (Longitudinal Data)
Combines cross-section and time series: $N$ units observed over $T$ periods, yielding $N \times T$ observations.

- Notation: $(y_{it}, x_{it})$ for unit $i$ at time $t$.
- Extremely powerful because repeated observations on the **same unit** allow control for unobserved unit-level heterogeneity (fixed effects, random effects).

## Causality vs. Correlation: Core Challenge

The fundamental challenge of econometrics: **correlation does not imply causation**.

- A positive correlation between police presence and crime could reflect *reverse causation* (more crime → more police deployed).
- Econometrics attempts to isolate **ceteris paribus** effects: what is the effect of $x_j$ on $y$ holding all other factors fixed?

The ceteris paribus interpretation is achievable with regression **only if** the error term $u$ is uncorrelated with all explanatory variables (MLR.4 / SLR.4 zero conditional mean assumption).

---

## Population Regression Function

The **Population Regression Function (PRF)** defines the expected value of $y$ conditional on $x$:

$$E(y \mid x) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x,$$

which traces the average $y$ for each value of $x$ in the population. It is a fixed, unknown object. Contrast with the **Sample Regression Function (SRF)**, which is estimated from data and changes across samples.

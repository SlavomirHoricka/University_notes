# Industry Equilibrium

## Market Demand Curve

The **market demand curve** is obtained by **horizontal summation** of individual demand curves: for a given price $p$, sum all quantities demanded across consumers.

**Long-run demand is more elastic than short-run demand:** In the long run, consumers can make more adjustments (substitute goods, change technology of consumption), amplifying their price response.

---

## Market Supply Curves

### Short-Run Market Supply

The **short-run market supply curve** is the **horizontal sum** of individual firm supply curves (for a fixed number of firms $n$):

$$Y^{SR}(p) = \sum_{i=1}^n S_i^{SR}(p)$$

**Long-run supply is more elastic than short-run supply:** In the long run, firms can adjust all inputs, and the number of firms also adjusts.

---

## Short-Run Equilibrium

**Short-run market equilibrium:** price $p^{SR}$ equalises market supply and demand:

$$Y^{SR}(p^{SR}) = D(p^{SR})$$

In the short run:
- The number of firms is **fixed**.
- Firms may earn positive or negative profits.
- Individual firm profits: $\pi_i = p^{SR} \cdot y_i - TC_i(y_i)$.

---

## Long-Run Equilibrium

### Free Entry and Exit

In a **free-entry industry**:
- **Positive profits** → entry of new firms → supply shifts right → price falls.
- **Negative profits** → exit of existing firms → supply shifts left → price rises.
- **Long-run equilibrium:** entry/exit continues until $\pi = 0$.

**Long-run equilibrium condition:**

$$p^{LR} = \min(ATC) \quad \text{and} \quad D(p^{LR}) = n^{LR} \cdot y^{LR}$$

where $n^{LR}$ is the equilibrium number of firms and $y^{LR}$ is each firm's efficient scale (output at $\min(ATC)$).

---

## Long-Run Market Supply Curve

The long-run supply curve is **not** the horizontal sum of individual long-run supply curves, because entry and exit change the number of firms.

### Case 1 — Identical Firms (U-shaped LRATC)

- $p^{LR} = \min(LRATC)$ — determined by technology.
- **Long-run supply curve is horizontal** at $p^* = \min(LRATC)$.
- Quantity supplied is **demand-determined** (adjusts entirely through entry/exit).

### Case 2 — Convex LTC (LMC and LRATC always increasing)

- Infinite number of firms, each producing an infinitesimally small output.
- Long-run supply curve is upward-sloping.

### Case 3 — Concave LTC (LMC and LRATC always decreasing — IRS)

- The industry tends toward **monopoly** (see [[Monopoly]]).

## Comparative Statics: Taxes

**Key result:** It does not matter whether a per-unit tax $t$ is levied on producers or consumers — the **incidence** (who actually bears the burden) is determined by supply and demand elasticities, not by the statutory incidence.

**Tax incidence formulae:**

Let $\varepsilon_S = dY/dp \cdot p/Y$ (supply elasticity) and $\varepsilon_D = dD/dp \cdot p/D$ (demand elasticity) in absolute value.

$$\text{Share borne by consumers} = \frac{\varepsilon_S}{\varepsilon_S + |\varepsilon_D|}$$

$$\text{Share borne by producers} = \frac{|\varepsilon_D|}{\varepsilon_S + |\varepsilon_D|}$$

**Deadweight loss (DWL):** The welfare cost of a tax — the area of the triangle between the supply, demand, and tax wedge:

$$DWL = \frac{1}{2} \cdot t \cdot \Delta Y$$

where $\Delta Y$ is the reduction in traded quantity.

**Practical implication — Drug Policy:** If demand for drugs is **inelastic** and supply is **elastic**, a reduction in supply (interdiction) raises prices but barely reduces quantity, largely burdening consumers (addicts). Reducing demand (treatment, education) is more effective.

## Economic Rents

Some factors of production are **inelastically supplied** (fixed):
- By nature: land, natural resources, unique talent
- Administratively: taxi licences, liquor licences

**Economic rent** = payment to a factor **in excess** of the minimum required to keep it employed.

$$\text{Economic rent} = \text{Producer surplus}$$

**Rent-seeking:** Firms invest resources to acquire rent-generating fixed factors (lobbying, legal fees). These expenditures represent **pure social waste** (deadweight loss) since they redistribute rather than create value.

### Equilibrium with Vertical Supply (Inelastic Factor)

- **Price determined by demand** (the scarce factor earns rents).
- **Quantity determined by supply** (fixed by nature or administratively).

### Equilibrium with Horizontal Supply (Elastic Factor)

- **Price determined by supply** (competitive factor market).
- **Quantity determined by demand**.

## Efficiency of Perfect Competition

Under a set of standard conditions (no externalities, no market power, no public goods, no information asymmetries), **perfect competition achieves Pareto efficiency**:

- Producer surplus is maximised given the competitive constraint.
- Consumer surplus is maximised given equilibrium price.
- Total surplus = consumer surplus + producer surplus is maximised.
- The market allocation exhausts all gains from trade; no deadweight loss.

This forms the welfare benchmark against which market failures and policy interventions are evaluated in the rest of the course (see [[Monopoly]], [[Price_Discrimination]], [[Oligopoly_Models]]).

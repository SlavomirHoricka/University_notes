# Accounting Framework and Financial Statements

## Definition

Accounting is an **information system** that measures business activities, processes them into reports, and communicates results to decision-makers. Financial accounting specifically produces general-purpose statements for external users (investors, creditors, regulators).

The four core financial statements are:

| Statement | Abbreviation | Answers |
|---|---|---|
| Balance Sheet | BS | What does the firm own and owe at a point in time? |
| Income Statement | IS | How much value did the firm generate over a period? |
| Statement of Cash Flows | SCF | How much cash did the firm generate/use over a period? |
| Statement of Equity | SE | How did owners' equity change over a period? |

## The Accounting Equation

$$\text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Equity}$$

This identity holds at every point in time and after every transaction. It is the structural constraint that makes double-entry bookkeeping internally consistent.

### Articulation Between Statements

The four statements are *articulated* — they flow into each other:

1. **IS** produces Net Income ($NI$) for the period.
2. **SE** takes $NI$ and subtracts dividends ($Dv$) to update Retained Earnings:

$$ARE_t = ARE_{t-1} + NI_t - Dv_t$$

3. **BS** reflects the updated equity.
4. **SCF** reconciles the opening and closing cash balances on the BS.

## T-Account Mechanics

Every account is represented as a **T-account**. The left side is the **debit** (Dr) side; the right side is the **credit** (Cr) side.

### Debit/Credit Rules

| Account Type | Increases on | Decreases on |
|---|---|---|
| Assets | Debit | Credit |
| Expenses | Debit | Credit |
| Liabilities | Credit | Debit |
| Equity | Credit | Debit |
| Revenues | Credit | Debit |

The mnemonic: **DEAL** accounts increase on the Debit side (Dividends, Expenses, Assets, Losses); **GIRLS** accounts increase on the Credit side (Gains, Income/Revenues, Liabilities, Stockholders' equity).

## Double-Entry Bookkeeping

Every transaction is recorded as at least one debit entry and one credit entry such that:

$$\sum \text{Debits} = \sum \text{Credits}$$

This ensures the accounting equation remains balanced after every transaction.

### Example — Credit Sale

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Accounts Receivable | $+X$ | |
| Sales Revenue | | $+X$ |
| Cost of Goods Sold | $+\text{COGS}$ | |
| Inventory | | $-\text{COGS}$ |

The sale is recorded at selling price (top pair) while the cost is simultaneously expensed (bottom pair).

## Users of Accounting Information

| User | Primary Need |
|---|---|
| Owners / Investors | Return and risk assessment |
| Creditors / Banks | Solvency and liquidity |
| Managers | Internal performance monitoring |
| Tax authorities | Taxable income determination |
| Regulators | Compliance verification |

## Accounting Types

| Type | Audience | Standard-setter | Key characteristic |
|---|---|---|---|
| Financial accounting | External | IFRS / US GAAP | Reliability + comparability |
| Tax accounting | Tax authority | National tax law | Cash basis, taxable income |
| Managerial accounting | Internal management | No external standard | Relevance for decisions |

### Reliability vs. Relevance Trade-off

Financial accounting standards balance two competing qualities:
- **Reliability (Verifiability):** information is free from bias and can be independently verified.
- **Relevance:** information is timely and affects user decisions.

Historical cost is highly reliable but may lack relevance; fair value is relevant but harder to verify.

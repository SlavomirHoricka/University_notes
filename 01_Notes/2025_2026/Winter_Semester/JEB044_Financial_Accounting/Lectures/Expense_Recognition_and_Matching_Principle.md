# Expense Recognition and the Matching Principle

## The Matching Principle (IFRS)

> Expenses are recognised in the income statement on the basis of a **direct association** between the costs incurred and the earning of specific items of income.

In plain terms: an expense is recognised in the same period as the revenue it helped generate. This is the accrual-basis counterpart to revenue recognition.

## Product Costs vs. Period Costs

| Cost Type | Definition | When Expensed | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Product cost (COGS)** | Costs directly attached to the goods produced or purchased | When the goods are sold (matched to revenue) | Raw materials, direct labour, manufacturing overhead |
| **Period cost (SG&A)** | Costs that cannot be traced to specific units of output | In the period incurred, regardless of sales | Marketing, executive salaries, rent, R&D |

The distinction determines whether a cost is initially **capitalised** (stored on the BS as inventory) or **expensed immediately** (flows directly to the IS).

### Inventory as a Cost Reservoir

Product costs "travel" through the balance sheet:

$$\underbrace{\text{Cash / Payables}}_{\text{outflow}} \xrightarrow{\text{purchase}} \underbrace{\text{Inventory}}_{\text{BS asset}} \xrightarrow{\text{sale}} \underbrace{COGS}_{\text{IS expense}}$$

The income statement never sees a product cost until the matching event (a sale) occurs.

## Operating Income Decomposition

$$EBIT = NS - COGS - SG\&A - \text{Other Operating Items}$$

Breaking COGS and SG&A apart allows analysis of:
- **Gross margin** ($GP/NS$): efficiency of production/purchasing.
- **Operating margin** ($EBIT/NS$): efficiency of the whole operating business.

## Matching Violations

Certain costs resist precise matching and are subject to explicit accounting rules:

| Cost | Treatment | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Research & Development (R&D) | Expensed immediately (IFRS: development costs may be capitalised if criteria met) | Future benefit too uncertain to capitalise reliably |
| Advertising | Expensed when incurred | No reliable link to specific future revenues |
| Depreciation | Systematic allocation over useful life | Matches the consumption of a long-lived asset against the revenues it generates |

**Insufficient depreciation** is a classic matching violation: if a firm under-depreciates PP&E, current-period expenses are understated and profits are overstated.

## Depreciation as a Non-Cash Expense

Depreciation reduces the carrying amount of a fixed asset:

$$\text{Net PP\&E}_t = \text{Gross PP\&E}_t - \text{Accumulated Depreciation}_t$$

Straight-line depreciation:

$$\text{Annual Depreciation} = \frac{\text{Cost} - \text{Residual Value}}{\text{Useful Life}}$$

Because depreciation reduces NI but involves no cash outflow, it must be **added back** when converting NI to operating cash flow in the indirect method (see [[Statement_of_Cash_Flows]]).

## Accruals as the Engine of Matching

Matching requires recognising costs in the correct period even when cash moves at a different time. This produces **accruals** and **deferrals** — temporary balance sheet accounts that bridge the timing gap. The full taxonomy is covered in [[Accruals_and_Deferrals]].

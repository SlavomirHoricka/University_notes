# Sigma-Algebra ($\sigma$-algebra)

## Motivation

To define probability on uncountable sample spaces (e.g., $S = \mathbb{R}$), we cannot assign probabilities to **every** subset of $S$. The system of all subsets of an uncountable $S$ is "too rich": it contains pathological sets (Vitali sets) for which no coherent probability assignment exists. We must restrict attention to a well-behaved **class of subsets** closed under the basic set operations.

**Definition 9 (Closure under an operation):** A class $\mathcal{A}$ of subsets of $S$ is **closed under** a given operation if performing that operation on elements of $\mathcal{A}$ always yields another element of $\mathcal{A}$.

## Definition

**Definition 10 ($\sigma$-algebra / $\sigma$-field):** Let $S$ be a nonempty set. A nonempty class $\mathcal{A}$ of subsets of $S$ is called a $\sigma$-algebra if:

1. $A \in \mathcal{A} \Rightarrow A^c \in \mathcal{A}$ — **closed under complements**
2. $A_n \in \mathcal{A},\; n = 1,2,\dots \Rightarrow \bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} A_n \in \mathcal{A}$ — **closed under countable unions**

Closeness under countable intersections follows automatically from De Morgan's laws.

### Remarks

- Any $\sigma$-algebra must contain $\emptyset$ and $S$ (since $\mathcal{A}$ is nonempty, take any $A \in \mathcal{A}$; then $A^c \in \mathcal{A}$ and $A \cup A^c = S \in \mathcal{A}$; and $S^c = \emptyset \in \mathcal{A}$).
- For a finite or countable $S$, we may take $\mathcal{A}$ to be the **power set** (class of all subsets) without trouble.
- For uncountable $S$, using the power set leads to "nonevents" — sets that cannot be assigned a well-defined length/measure.

## Extreme Cases (Definition 11)

**Smallest $\sigma$-algebra on $S$:** $\mathcal{A} = \{\emptyset, S\}$ — contains only the impossible and sure events.

**Largest (maximal) $\sigma$-algebra on $S$:** $\mathcal{A} = \mathcal{P}(S)$ — the power set of $S$ (all subsets). Fine for countable $S$, problematic for uncountable $S$.

For any event $A$, the **smallest $\sigma$-algebra containing $A$** is $\{\emptyset, A, A^c, S\}$.

## Generated $\sigma$-algebra

**Theorem 2 (Existence, without proof):** For any nonempty class $\mathcal{K}$ of subsets of $S$, there exists a unique **smallest** $\sigma$-algebra containing all sets in $\mathcal{K}$, denoted $\sigma(\mathcal{K})$ and called the **$\sigma$-algebra generated by $\mathcal{K}$**.

## Borel $\sigma$-algebra

The $\sigma$-algebra generated by all **open intervals** on $\mathbb{R}$ is called the **Borel $\sigma$-algebra** $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})$.

- It contains all open sets, closed sets, and their countable combinations — everything we would ever want to call a "measurable event" on the real line.
- It excludes pathological sets like Vitali sets that arise from the axiom of choice.
- The maximal $\sigma$-algebra on $\mathbb{R}$ (power set) is "too rich" and contains nonevents.

## Role in Probability Theory

The $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{A}$ specifies the **domain** of the probability function $P$. The triple $(S, \mathcal{A}, P)$ is the probability space (see [[Kolmogorov_Axioms]]). Restricting $P$ to a $\sigma$-algebra ensures that $P$ is countably additive and well-defined on all "events" of interest, without encountering measure-theoretic paradoxes.

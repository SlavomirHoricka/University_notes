## Motivation and explanation

A futures price F=100−i uses i as an annual percentage, not a decimal fraction. A one-point price change is a one-percentage-point rate move in the opposite direction. A0.01 quote-point tick represents0.0001 annual decimal, so principal L and accrual fraction τ imply tick value L×.0001×τ. A lender loses spot interest when rates fall; a long future then gains because F rises. For matching principal, undiscounted effective interest cash is L×i_spot×τ+G, so i_effective=i_spot+G/(L×τ). A consistent quarter-year tick and matching accrual .25 produce the initial implied rate. The notes identify a different91/365 fraction and payment-timing inconsistency; do not silently mix them or equate this simple algebra to an exact hedge including all daily cash-flow discounting. A physically arranged rate lower than the benchmark requires the separate difference ×principal×specified accrual compensation; the benchmark and received rate are distinct.

## Worked example

O011-W1 / item_version 1, worked_example. **Prompt:** Hypothetical three-month IR future: EUR500000 principal, F=100−annual rate in percent, tick0.01 quote point, agreed quarter-year fraction0.25, initial margin EUR500 per contract. A lender/depositor buys 1 at 90.50; it could close at 90.55 after 14 days or instead hold to price EDSP 91.25. Derive tick value and position direction; compute close P/L, margin return and simple365-day annualisation, held P/L, annual spot rate and effective deposit rate for matching total principal. For alternative physical placement at a rate0.05 percentage point below the benchmark, calculate separate compensation using the same0.25 fraction, add it to the actual interest and the held futures gain, and derive the final effective rate. Derive the rate adjustment from cash flows. Explain why using91/365 instead changes the result and why the illustration is not a universal discounted daily-futures hedge.

**Steps and interpretation:** Tick in rate is0.01 percentage point=.0001 annual decimal. Tick money=500000×.0001×.25=EUR12.50. Price rises when rate falls, so long hedges a lender against falling deposit rates; a borrower exposed to rising rates takes short. Close G=1×12.5×(90.55−90.50)/.01=EUR62.500000; margin return 12.5%, annualised 325.893%. Held G=EUR937.500000; spot rate=(100−91.25)/100=0.087500 annual decimal. Matching principal EUR500000, cash interest L×spot×.25; add G and divide by L×.25 gives effective rate 9.5%, the entry-implied 9.50%. Physical received rate=8.7%; compensation=L×(i_benchmark−i_received)×.25=EUR62.500000. Actual interest=L×0.087000×.25 plus compensation restores benchmark interest; adding held futures G then gives the same9.5% effective annual rate. Futures gain and placement compensation are distinct flows, not the same payment counted twice. This uses one consistent quarter-year convention and undiscounted comparison. Replacing only denominator .25 with91/365 mixes conventions and slightly changes the effective rate; explicit all-leg day-count/timing treatment is needed. The note records unreconciled source discounted/non-discounted expressions, so no universal exact daily-settled hedge or margin-account value is claimed. Close and held paths are alternative outcomes.

## Misconceptions and correction

A correct gain with a wrong effective rate often arises from an inconsistent accrual denominator. State each rate in annual decimal and each time fraction before adding money to interest. Observe the learner's reasoning before diagnosing: a sign or omitted word alone is tentative. Score before help. O011-C1 / item_version 1, narrow_correction. **Fresh prompt:** Fresh narrow check: a matched lender has spot interest rate4%, principal200000, τ.25 and futures gain500. Effective annual decimal? **Key and rubric:** (.04×200000×.25+500)/(200000×.25)=.05=5%; money500 must first be divided by principal×time. Pass only if the keyed repaired component is supplied; partial for an omission or arithmetic slip; fail for the same conflicting mechanism. C1 is not a full independent check.

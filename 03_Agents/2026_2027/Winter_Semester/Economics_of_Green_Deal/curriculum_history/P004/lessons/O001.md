## Motivation, intuition, and definitions

A forest can supply timber, enjoyment and systemic services. Asking only what it sells misses other ways it matters. A function describes a relationship, so the same site can have several functions.

Terms and symbols are defined in the explanation below and exact cited sections. In network lessons P=production, C=consumption, U=utility, R=natural resources, ER=exhaustible resources, RR=renewable resources, h=harvest rate, y=yield/growth, W=waste, A=assimilative capacity. These are qualitative labels; units are supplied explicitly only in our hypothetical comparisons.

## Explanation steps

1. Ask whether the environmental feature supplies an input or possibility for economic activity: this is a resource. Wood/biomass are degradable renewable resources; fossil fuels/minerals are degradable non-renewable/exhaustible resources. Sunshine, wind and waves are separately classified as non-degradable in this source; space is also a resource.
2. Ask whether the relationship concerns receiving undesirable outputs: this is the sink function, interpreted through assimilative capacity. A sink does not imply unlimited capacity.
3. Ask whether people value aesthetic, cultural or recreational services directly: these are demand services. Forest recreation differs from timber production even at the same site.
4. Ask whether the relationship concerns broad life-supporting systems: climate, the hydrologic cycle and soil production belong to the biospheric category.
5. Keep categories distinct by mechanism while allowing coexistence. The source supplies this taxonomy, not a general formal definition of degradability; do not replace its wording with another taxonomy.

### Four environmental functions

The slides distinguish four ways in which the environment matters to the economy. These functions can coexist in the same place. The forest example shows the distinction particularly clearly: wood is a resource, while aesthetic, cultural and recreational enjoyment belongs to the separate category of demand services. This comparison is an explanatory synthesis of the examples on slide 2. [[00_Materials/2026_2027/Winter_Semester/Economics_of_Green_Deal/Week_1/00_Intro.pptx|00_Intro.pptx, slide 2]]

#### Resources

Resources provide inputs or possibilities for economic activity. Slide 2 distinguishes **non-degradable resources**, with sunshine, wind and waves as examples, from **degradable resources**. Within the latter group it distinguishes renewable resources, such as wood and biomass, from non-renewable or exhaustible resources, such as fossil fuels and minerals. It also lists space as a resource. Keep this source taxonomy when interpreting the later diagram: the slide uses “renewable” for the wood/biomass category and separately lists sunshine, wind and waves as non-degradable. It does not supply a broader taxonomy or a formal definition of “degradable.” [[00_Materials/2026_2027/Winter_Semester/Economics_of_Green_Deal/Week_1/00_Intro.pptx|00_Intro.pptx, slide 2]]

The distinction becomes economically meaningful when resource use is compared with replenishment. An exhaustible resource can be reduced by extraction. For renewable resources, the diagram separately represents harvesting above and below yield or growth, so “renewable” alone does not establish that a particular pattern of use maintains the resource. The relevant question is how fast the resource is used relative to how fast it grows. [[00_Materials/2026_2027/Winter_Semester/Economics_of_Green_Deal/Week_1/00_Intro.pptx|00_Intro.pptx, slides 2 and 4]]

#### Sinks and assimilative capacity

A **sink** is a place where undesirable outputs, including emissions and wastes, may be placed. The source describes this function through **assimilative capacity**: the environment's capacity to receive or assimilate those outputs. The diagram later compares waste with that capacity. This makes waste disposal part of the environment–economy relationship, alongside resource extraction. [[00_Materials/2026_2027/Winter_Semester/Economics_of_Green_Deal/Week_1/00_Intro.pptx|00_Intro.pptx, slides 2 and 4]]

The capacity comparison matters because the diagram assigns different consequences to waste below capacity and waste above capacity. The existence of a sink therefore does not establish that every amount of waste has the same consequences. The source supplies a qualitative threshold comparison; it does not quantify a safe emissions level, a pollutant-specific assimilation rate, or the time needed for assimilation. [[00_Materials/2026_2027/Winter_Semester/Economics_of_Green_Deal/Week_1/00_Intro.pptx|00_Intro.pptx, slide 4]]

#### Demand services

The environment also supplies services people value directly. Slide 2 names aesthetic, cultural and recreational services, such as those provided by a forest. In the full diagram, the environmental resource node has a positive amenity connection to utility. Thus the environment contributes to wellbeing through more than goods produced by firms and consumed by households. [[00_Materials/2026_2027/Winter_Semester/Economics_of_Green_Deal/Week_1/00_Intro.pptx|00_Intro.pptx, slides 2 and 4]]

**Utility**, denoted by $U$, is the diagram's representation of wellbeing or benefit. The source identifies utility flows but gives no numerical utility scale, utility function, or valuation method. The positive amenity arrow establishes a direction of effect in this schematic framework; it does not report a measured monetary benefit. [[00_Materials/2026_2027/Winter_Semester/Economics_of_Green_Deal/Week_1/00_Intro.pptx|00_Intro.pptx, slide 4]]

#### Global biospheric life-supporting services

The fourth category concerns the systems that support life more generally. The source names the climate system, hydrologic cycle and soil production. These services place the production-and-consumption relationship in a wider biospheric setting. They explain why environmental economics considers systemic conditions as well as individual resource inputs and enjoyable amenities. This final sentence is a synthesis of the category's role in the introductory framework. [[00_Materials/2026_2027/Winter_Semester/Economics_of_Green_Deal/Week_1/00_Intro.pptx|00_Intro.pptx, slide 2]]


## Worked example

**O001-W1 v1, instruction only.** An agent-created forest example combines timber, recreation, receipt of undesirable outputs, and the hydrologic cycle. Classify each and compare wood, fossil fuels and wind in the source taxonomy.

**Worked solution:** Step 1: timber supplies an input, so resource. Step 2: recreation gives direct enjoyment, so demand service. Step 3: receipt/assimilation of outputs is sink service, without an unlimited-capacity claim. Step 4: the hydrologic cycle is a biospheric life-supporting service. Wood is degradable renewable; fossil fuels are degradable exhaustible; wind is non-degradable in the slide. The same place supports several relationships; its identity does not force one exclusive category.

Sign: all environmental benefits are called resources, or sunshine is silently recategorized. Treat one response as tentative; a repeated conflation across fresh tasks supports diagnosis. Correction: identify the relationship (input, waste reception, direct enjoyment, life support), then retain the slide’s labels. Hint 1: ask what role each feature plays. Hint 2: contrast timber with recreation at the same forest.

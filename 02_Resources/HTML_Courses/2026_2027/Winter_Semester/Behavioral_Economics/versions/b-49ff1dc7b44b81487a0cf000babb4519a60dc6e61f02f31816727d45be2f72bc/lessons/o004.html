03

Motivation, intuition, and definitions

Reciprocity conditions concern for another person on what that person did. Positive payoff concern can also be unconditional; inequality concern instead depends on who is ahead. Efficiency concerns the monetary sum. Similar observed transfers need not identify which motivation is operating.

Visual support:

No visual asset is necessary for this objective; use the explicit equations, labels and reasoning prepared here.

04

Explanation steps

  1. In the displayed reciprocity equation define \(w(q)=\rho+\theta q\): utility is \(w(q)x_2+[1-w(q)]x_1\). Kindness gives \(w_+=\rho+\theta\), misbehavior \(w_-=\rho-\theta\), so \(w_+-w_-=2\theta>0\).
  2. Negative total weight after misbehavior requires \(\rho-\theta<0\), equivalently \(\theta>\rho\). A positive \(\theta\) alone cannot establish hostility; baseline \(\rho\) can be larger. At \(\theta =\rho\) the negative-treatment weight is zero.
  3. Identical allocations can have different utilities because \(q\) changes; this differs from unconditional altruism and inequality-only weighting. Do not say kindness raises utility for every fixed allocation: \(U_+-U_-=2\theta(x_2-x_1)\), so the sign depends on relative monetary payoffs. This is direct unpacking of the supplied equation, not an estimated treatment effect.
  4. As the notes’ labelled illustration, set both earlier payoff weights to \(1/2\): \(U=(x_1+x_2)/2\). Ranking this particular utility is equivalent to ranking the total. This supplies an illustration of efficiency concern within the existing function, not a complete separately estimated efficiency-seeking model or evidence that all efficiency preferences use these weights.
  5. Sequential trust lets a trustee observe the prior transfer; simultaneous cooperation requires a belief about current kindness before either action is seen. The lecture names positive reciprocity plus belief in cooperation but gives no separate quantitative simultaneous mechanism, measured intention rule, study rate or belief threshold. A single transfer cannot isolate the motivation.

Reciprocity responds to prior treatment

Reciprocity means kindness toward someone who acted kindly and hostility toward someone who acted unkindly. The source adds \(q=1\) for person 2's kind action and \(q=-1\) for misbehavior, with sensitivity \(\theta>0\):

\[U_1(x_1,x_2)=(\rho+\theta q)x_2+(1-\rho-\theta q)x_1.\]

Define the effective social weight \(w(q)=\rho+\theta q\). A kind action gives \(w(1)=\rho+\theta\); misbehavior gives \(w(-1)=\rho-\theta\). Thus treatment changes the weight on the other person's payoff by \(2\theta\) between these two states. A negative response weight follows if \(\theta>\rho\); \(\theta>0\) alone does not ensure that the total weight becomes negative. These comparisons unpack the supplied equation. Social Preferences, physical PDF p. 22

Reciprocity therefore differs from unconditional altruism and from payoff inequality alone. Two identical monetary allocations can have different utility if prior treatment changes \(q\). The lecture defines the two kindness states but supplies no detailed rule for measuring perceived intentions or estimating \(\theta\). The displayed equation is a simple illustration, not a full solution of every strategic game or a replacement of the earlier two-regime function in all contexts. Social Preferences, physical PDF pp. 22, 27, 36

Efficiency-seeking and the welfare connection

The lecture names efficiency-seeking alongside the other social preferences and lists it as a possible reason for trustor transfers. Its relevant intuition is concern for the total monetary surplus that cooperation can create. The presentation does not give a separate complete efficiency-seeking utility function or identify it from one observed transfer. Social Preferences, physical PDF pp. 12, 27, 35, 37

Agent-created illustration using the displayed model: \(\rho=\sigma=1/2\) yields \(U_1=(x_1+x_2)/2\), so maximizing this particular utility is equivalent to maximizing total monetary payoff. This illustrates how the existing payoff weights can value a larger total; it is not a claim that the lecture estimates those parameters or that all efficiency-seeking preferences take this exact form. Concern about totals, distribution and kind treatment can produce similar choices in some games, making the experimental comparison important. Social Preferences, physical PDF pp. 14, 27

Trust and cooperation games develops the distinction between splitting a fixed pie and expanding monetary surplus. Social preferences can encourage the latter, while beliefs and incentives still determine whether cooperation occurs. The central result of these models is a set of distinct mechanisms—positive payoff weights, negative payoff weights, disparity penalties and treatment-dependent weights—not a single universal “unselfish” preference. Social Preferences, physical PDF pp. 23–24, 35–37

Reciprocity needs an expectation about the other player

The lecture's second explanation is positive reciprocity combined with the belief that the other person will cooperate. It cites Dawes and Thaler (1988) beside the observation that cooperation occurs even with a finite number of interactions, sometimes only once; the uploaded slide does not supply the underlying study design, sample, or numerical cooperation rate. Positive reciprocity means wanting to respond kindly to perceived kindness. In a simultaneous game there is no observed current action to reciprocate at the moment of choice, so an expectation about the other's cooperation is part of this proposed explanation. The lecture does not specify a separate payoff formula or a quantitative belief threshold for this mechanism. Social Preferences, physical PDF p. 36

This differs from the trust game, where the trustee observes the initial transfer before returning money. It also differs from the unconditional warm-glow premium: a desire to reward kindness depends on how the other player's behavior is perceived or expected. The reciprocity model makes this dependence explicit. Social Preferences, physical PDF p. 22 Social Preferences, physical PDF p. 36; connection between games

Misconceptions and correction

Signs: \(\theta >0\) automatically produces a negative weight; kindness always increases utility; half-total illustration called the complete efficiency model. Correction: calculate \(\rho -\theta\) and distinguish weight change from utility change; retain the illustration label and model limits. Hint1: subtract \(\theta\) from baseline \(\rho\). Hint2: multiply the weight change by \(x_2-x_1\). C1 is repair only.

A sign is a tentative hypothesis about the error until the learner’s reasoning supports it. Score the initial response before giving the corrective explanation or either hint. At most two ineffective hints, then show the specific prepared step or pause. After two unsuccessful full independent attempts, stop quizzing and use support/prerequisite repair; request new full variants from plan if needed.

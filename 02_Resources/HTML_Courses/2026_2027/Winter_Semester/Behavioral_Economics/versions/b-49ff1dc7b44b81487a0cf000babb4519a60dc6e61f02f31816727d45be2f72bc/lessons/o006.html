03

Motivation, intuition, and definitions

Two people each choosing privately can fail to implement an outcome that would improve both. Simultaneous means the current other action is unknown at the choice moment. A Nash profile is stable against one player changing alone, not merely attractive to both as a joint move.

Visual support:

  • Social Preferences p32 Prisoners Dilemma — inspected qualitative/source support; retrieve the finished note’s adjacent explanation.
04

Explanation steps

  1. Symmetric monetary matrix: CC \((a,a)\),CD \((d,c)\),DC \((c,d)\),DD \((b,b)\), with \(c>a>b>d\). A compares first coordinates; B second. Anonymous players cannot communicate in the described setup.
  2. Against C, defect yields \(c>a\); against D, defect yields \(b>d\). D strictly dominates for A and by symmetry B. Therefore DD is the unique Nash equilibrium: either other action still makes D strictly optimal; no probability mixture can reverse a strict comparison. At CC, a player gains \(c-a\) by unilateral D. At DD, switching alone loses \(b-d\).
  3. Mutual cooperation gives each \(a>b\), so both are better than DD. However total in CC is \(2a\), in DD \(2b\) and in a mixed cellc+d. \(c>a>b>d\) alone does not order \(2a\) againstc+d. A claim that CC maximizes every monetary total needs that additional comparison, absent from general source assumptions.
  4. For rational self-regarding players with commonly known finite final period, fixed stage game and additive stage payoffs (possibly positive discounted weights), last-period D is optimal after every history because no future incentive remains. Last-period D is fixed regardless of penultimate choices, so the penultimate current comparison gives D; continue backwards to first. The unique subgame-perfect benchmark defects at every stage after every history, not just on the realized path.
  5. “They will meet again” does not overturn this known-finite logic. The proof does not cover uncertain/indefinite horizon, uncertainty about preferences or changed social preferences. Lecture observed cooperation motivates amendments; it supplies no poll responses or precise rate here.

The prisoner's dilemma: a simultaneous conflict

In the lecture's prisoner's dilemma, two anonymous players cannot communicate and choose cooperate or defect simultaneously. Simultaneous means each chooses without knowing the other's current action. The first number in each payoff pair belongs to Player A, who chooses the row; the second belongs to Player B, who chooses the column. The monetary units are not specified on the numerical slide. Social Preferences, physical PDF pp. 29–31

Player A's action B cooperates B defects
Cooperate \((16,16)\) \((8,20)\)
Defect \((20,8)\) \((12,12)\)

The full source slide preserves the numerical matrix and the lecture's dominant-strategy and Nash-equilibrium claims. Social Preferences, physical PDF p. 32

Dominance and Nash equilibrium

A strictly dominant strategy gives a player a higher payoff than the alternative for every action of the other player. Agent unpacking of the matrix: if B cooperates, A gets 20 from defecting rather than 16 from cooperating; if B defects, A gets 12 rather than 8. Defection is therefore strictly dominant for A. By symmetry, it is strictly dominant for B too. A Nash equilibrium is a pair of strategies in which neither player benefits from changing their own choice while holding the other's fixed. Because each player's strict best response is always defection, \((D,D)\) is the unique Nash equilibrium under self-regarding preferences. The classroom question on PDF p. 30 has no displayed poll results. Social Preferences, physical PDF pp. 29–32

Each receives 12 in equilibrium, although mutual cooperation would give each 16. The difference is a conflict between the individually attractive deviation and the jointly preferable cooperative outcome. From \((C,C)\), either player can gain 4 by defecting alone. From \((D,D)\), switching alone to cooperation loses 4. Thus joint improvement does not make unilateral cooperation individually optimal. This illustrates the lecture's argument that privately costly cooperation can increase social welfare while a private incentive points toward defection. Social Preferences, physical PDF pp. 31–32; payoff interpretation

General payoffs and finite repetition

The lecture's general symmetric matrix has \(c>a>b>d\):

Player A's action B cooperates B defects
Cooperate \((a,a)\) \((d,c)\)
Defect \((c,d)\) \((b,b)\)

Here \(c\) is the payoff from defecting against cooperation, \(a\) the mutual-cooperation payoff, \(b\) the mutual-defection payoff, and \(d\) the payoff from cooperating against defection. The inequalities \(c>a\) and \(b>d\) make defection strictly dominant; \(a>b\) makes mutual cooperation better for both than mutual defection. These inequalities alone do not state that mutual cooperation maximizes the sum among every possible cell, since no condition comparing \(2a\) with \(c+d\) is given. Social Preferences, physical PDF pp. 33–34; matrix interpretation

The lecture states that the unique subgame-perfect equilibrium of a finitely repeated prisoner's dilemma is defection in every period. Agent unpacking of the required benchmark: take a fixed pair of rational, self-regarding players, a commonly known finite final period, the same stage game in every period, and payoffs given by the sum of the stage payoffs, possibly discounted by positive weights. In the last period there is no future reward or punishment left, so both defect after every possible preceding history. In the penultimate period, last-period defection will occur regardless of today's choices, so the current dominant-strategy logic again gives defection. Repeating this argument backward establishes defection at every stage and after every history. This is stronger than just specifying the actions on one realized path. Social Preferences, physical PDF p. 34; backward-induction explanation

The result is conditional on that benchmark, especially the known finite horizon and self-regarding preferences. A chance to meet again does not by itself overturn the backward-induction argument. The argument presented here does not cover an uncertain or indefinite horizon, uncertainty about the other player's preferences, or the social-preference changes introduced next. Social Preferences, physical PDF pp. 34–36; scope of the prediction

Worked example

O006-W1 — Source numerical dilemma

Item version: v1. Role: worked instruction.

Setup: Use the finished-note matrix with \(a=16, c=20, b=12, d=8\). Agent-created finite-horizon illustration: 3 commonly known periods.

Worked reasoning and result: The symmetric matrix is \(\mathrm{CC}=(16,16)\), \(\mathrm{CD}=(8,20)\), \(\mathrm{DC}=(20,8)\), \(\mathrm{DD}=(12,12)\), satisfying \(c>a>b>d\). A chooses rows and reads first coordinates; B chooses columns and reads second coordinates. Choices are simultaneous, anonymous and without communication. For each player, \(D\) gives \(20>16\) against \(C\) and \(12>8\) against \(D\). \(D\) strictly dominates; \((D,D)\) is the unique monetary Nash equilibrium, stable against unilateral changes. Defecting alone from \(\mathrm{CC}\) gains \(4\); switching alone from \(\mathrm{DD}\) to \(C\) loses \(4\). \(\mathrm{CC}\) gives each \(16>12\) compared with \(\mathrm{DD}\). Cell totals are \(\mathrm{CC}=32\), \(\mathrm{DD}=24\), \(\text{mixed}=28\); \(\mathrm{CC}\) has the largest total in this example. The general ordering alone does not compare \(2a\) with \(c+d\). In period \(3\), \(D\) is optimal after every history because no future incentive remains. That future defection is fixed regardless of the previous action, making \(D\) optimal in period \(2\); repeat backwards to period \(1\). This all-history argument requires rational self-regarding players, a commonly known fixed finite horizon, a fixed stage game and additive stage payoffs with positive weights. It does not cover uncertain/indefinite horizons or uncertain/changed preferences; merely meeting again does not overturn the proof. No classroom poll results or precise cooperation rate are supplied.

Checks and interpretation: Verify labels, active conditions, dimensions/signs where applicable and every source limitation before inferring a choice. This example is exposed instruction and cannot establish independence.

Misconceptions and correction

Signs: CC called Nash because both prefer it, or repetition alone said to ensure cooperation. Correction: hold the other action fixed and compare unilateral payoff; start from the known final stage after every history. Hint1: what if only A defects? Hint2: what future remains in finalperiod? C1 fresh repair only.

03

Key definitions and concepts

  • Ultimatum game: The receiver can accept the offered division or reject it, giving both players zero.
  • Dictator game: The proposer implements the division without a receiver veto.
  • Strategic giving: A positive offer motivated by the possibility of rejection rather than necessarily concern for the receiver.

3.1 Notation and distinctions

No visual asset is necessary for this objective; use the explicit equations, labels and reasoning prepared here.

04

Ultimatum and dictator games

Do people maximize only their own monetary payoff when choosing how to divide money? The ultimatum game and dictator game isolate different parts of this question.

In the ultimatum game, the recipient can reject an allocation; in the dictator game, the recipient has no such power.

Comparing them helps distinguish the effect of threatened rejection from willingness to give when rejection is impossible. Both are introduced as anonymous, incentivized laboratory decisions, rather than hypothetical evidence about what every person always does.

Return to . The experimental-method distinction is developed in Behavioral and experimental economics.

4.1 The ultimatum game's sequence and payoffs

The ultimatum game (UG) has two players, a proposer and a receiver. The proposer is given a fixed monetary pie and chooses a split.

The receiver then accepts or rejects. Acceptance implements the split;

rejection leaves both players with nothing. The source specifies a one-time, anonymous interaction: the receiver does not know, and will not learn, the proposer's identity.

Participants are paid according to their choices.

Notation and payoff accounting unpacked from the setup: let \(M>0\) be the pie and \(r\in[0,M]\) the amount offered to the receiver. Acceptance gives monetary payoffs

\[(x_P,x_R)=(M-r,r),\]

where \(P\) labels the proposer and \(R\) the receiver. Rejection gives \((0,0)\). Thus an offer is not a completed transfer: the receiver has a veto. Rejecting a positive offer is costly to the receiver as well as the proposer. This opportunity to impose a cost is the central difference from the dictator game.

The lecture's example uses \(M=\text{CZK }500\) and asks whether the receiver would accept offers of CZK 0, 1, 20, 100, 150, 200 or 250. The corresponding proposer shares are 500, 499, 480, 400, 350, 300 and 250. These are choice prompts, not observed decisions. The slides also ask for the receiver's minimum acceptable offer and the selfish-model prediction; the exported poll slides contain no responses or results.

4.2 Deriving the self-regarding benchmark

Self-regarding preferences make a player's utility depend only on that player's own monetary payoff. The benchmark also assumes the proposer expects the receiver to be self-regarding.

To solve the sequential game, reason from the receiver's decision backward to the proposer's offer. This is the lecture's comparison with a subgame-perfect equilibrium: a strategy prediction that remains optimal at the later decision stage, including after an offer has actually been made.

For any \(r>0\), a purely monetary receiver gets \(r\) from accepting and zero from rejecting, so acceptance is strictly better. A proposer who anticipates this wants to retain as much as possible and offer next to nothing. The lecture summarizes the prediction as an almost-zero offer that is accepted.

Qualification derived from the payoff comparison: at exactly \(r=0\), acceptance and rejection both give the receiver zero. Pure self-interest alone therefore leaves the receiver indifferent at zero.

An exact minimum offer depends on the allowed monetary unit and how that tie is resolved. If whole CZK offers are allowed and zero would be rejected, the smallest strictly profitable accepted offer is CZK 1.

If zero is accepted, offering zero can be optimal. The source supplies neither a tie rule nor a complete offer grid, so its “next to nothing” prediction should not be turned into an unconditional unique zero-offer result.

4.3 Typical ultimatum results and what they challenge

The lecture reports that the modal offer—the most common offer—is 40–50% of the pie, with very few offers below 20%. Offers below 20% are rejected very often, more than 50% of the time in the stated summary.

It says these patterns have been replicated many times and differ substantially from the self-regarding subgame-perfect prediction. These percentages are the lecture's reported typical laboratory patterns, not a class-poll result or a universal rate for every design and population.

The rejection of a positive amount is particularly informative: the receiver gives up money to prevent the unfavorable split from being implemented. Social-preference models explain how concern about relative payoffs or treatment can make such an action attractive even though it reduces monetary income. The observed action challenges the purely monetary benchmark under the stated setup;

it does not by itself identify one unique preference model.

A positive proposer offer is also not sufficient evidence of altruism. Reasoning from the game's veto: even a self-interested proposer can offer more when expecting a low offer to be rejected. The offer may reflect concern for the receiver, anticipation of costly rejection, or both. This is why the dictator game's removal of the veto is useful.

4.4 The dictator game removes the receiver's veto

The dictator game (DG) also has a proposer and receiver and a fixed pie, but the proposer’s chosen split is simply implemented. The receiver is completely passive.

There is no acceptance or rejection stage. With the same notation, the implemented monetary payoffs are \((M-r,r)\).

A strictly self-regarding proposer therefore chooses \(r=0\), because every positive transfer directly reduces their own payoff and cannot avoid a receiver veto.

Consider a typical laboratory implementation with a $10 pie supplied by the experimenter, participants randomly assigned to proposer and receiver roles, and anonymity about individual decisions or pairings. The endowment is received rather than earned. These procedural features matter when interpreting what the comparison does and does not hold constant.

The stated typical results are that more than 60% of participants transfer a positive amount, with a mean transfer of roughly 20% of the endowment. The fraction of people giving something and the average fraction of the pie given are different quantities: a 20% mean does not mean that 20% of participants give or that every giver transfers exactly 20%.

For the stated $10 endowment, 20% is about $2 as an arithmetic illustration, not a separately observed result.

The lecture stresses sensitivity to procedural details: when the endowment is earned rather than received from the experimenter, giving diminishes. It does not provide a numerical size for that reduction.

Positive giving therefore supplies evidence against complete monetary selfishness for many participants in the described setting, while its level depends on the experimental procedure. It does not establish that all participants are unselfish or that these laboratory averages directly predict giving in every other setting.

4.5 Reading the two games together

Feature Ultimatum game Dictator game
Who chooses the split? Proposer Proposer
Can the receiver reject? Yes; rejection gives both zero No; the split is implemented
Self-regarding proposer benchmark Offer next to nothing that will be accepted Give zero
What positive giving can reflect Social concern and/or anticipated rejection Giving without a receiver veto
What the source reports Substantial offers and frequent rejection of very low offers Many positive transfers; giving sensitive to the endowment procedure

The comparison isolates the receiver's strategic power, while the lecture's reported results show that many choices need a broader account of motivation. The next step is to define what “broader” means: Models of social preferences separates altruism, spite, inequality aversion and reciprocity. Trust and cooperation games then examines settings where behavior can change the total monetary surplus, instead of only dividing a fixed pie.

05

Connect the reasoning

  1. Write acceptance as \((M-r,r)\) and rejection as \((0,0)\). Compare the receiver’s own money last: \(r>0\) beats zero; at \(r=0\) both choices tie.
  2. The proposer anticipates this response and retains as much as feasible. “Next to nothing accepted” is the lecture benchmark. An exact zero or smallest positive offer requires the offer grid and a zero-offer tie rule. Whole units and rejection of zero imply offer 1; accepting zero permits offer 0. Do not invent a unique zero result without those assumptions.
  3. Remove the veto. DG now implements \((M-r,r)\) for any offer. A monetary maximizer chooses zero because \(M-r\) falls with \(r\); there is no receiver acceptance stage to anticipate.
  4. Lecture summaries: UG modal offer 40–50%; very few below 20%; offers below 20% often rejected more than 50%. DG more than 60% give positively and mean share is roughly 20%; received endowment versus earned changes giving, with earned giving diminishing but no supplied effect size. A modal amount, fraction of givers and mean share are different summaries. Empty polls give no additional results.
  5. Positive UG giving can insure against anticipated rejection; positive rejection sacrifices money and challenges the pure-money receiver benchmark. DG giving removes the veto explanation but does not isolate a unique preference. The lecture’s rates are approximate descriptive summaries, not universal individual choices or full causal estimates.
06

Worked example

6.1 — Veto comparison

Setup: Hypothetical: pie 80 units, receiver offered 8. Contrast UG and DG; assume whole units and UG receiver rejects zero but accepts every positive offer.

Worked reasoning and result: UG acceptance gives \((72,8)\); rejection \((0,0)\).

A selfish receiver strictly accepts \(8\); zero ties monetarily, so the extra tie rule matters.

Given whole units and reject-zero, selfish proposer offers \(1\), receiving \(79\). DG receiver has no veto and proposer gives \(0\), receiving \(80\).

Strategic UG giving can reflect fear of rejection; DG positive giving cannot avoid a nonexistent veto.

The lecture’s UG modal \(40\text{–}50\%\) corresponds to a range of shares, not every person; DG \(>60\%\) positive givers and \(\sim 20\%\) mean are separate quantities, and earned giving diminishes without a stated amount.

Choose one answer per question. Check individual answers or check the whole quiz. Each option includes feedback on the reasoning, and everything works offline.

8.1 Question 1

A pie is 80 units and the receiver is offered 8. What are ultimatum-game payoffs if the offer is accepted?

Reveal explanation

Subtract the offer from the pie for the proposer; the receiver obtains the offer.

8.2 Question 2

Whole units, a selfish receiver who rejects zero and accepts every positive offer: what does the selfish proposer offer?

Reveal explanation

With this explicit tie rule,1 is the smallest accepted offer. Zero would be rejected.

8.3 Question 3

Why can fear of rejection explain positive ultimatum offers but not dictator giving?

Reveal explanation

The monetary incentive to avoid rejection exists only when rejection can change the outcome.

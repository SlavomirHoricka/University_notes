02

Introduction

People sometimes overlook information, struggle to follow through on plans, or care about fairness alongside their own reward. How can economics explain these choices—and how can we test whether an explanation is right?

This lesson introduces behavioral economics and shows how its explanations connect to experimental evidence.

2.1 What you’ll learn

  • Explain behavioral economics as specific psychological changes to economic assumptions, with testable predictions.
  • Recognize all five introductory categories: motivations, self-control, attention, beliefs, and preference stability.
  • Distinguish a behavioral explanation from an experimental method.
  • Explain why correlation or a before-and-after change does not establish causation, and how experimental control helps without guaranteeing it.
  • Propose a falsifiable conditional prediction and connect theory, evidence, and a possible application without treating a hypothetical example as a result.

2.2 Before you begin

  • No earlier lesson in this course is required.
  • The course assumes Microeconomics I: you will compare behavioral explanations with conventional economic assumptions.
  • You do not need the formal models from later lectures for this introduction.
Quick check: prediction or evidence?

A model predicts an action; an observation records an action. Which is a prediction and which is evidence?

Reveal the answer

The model statement is a prediction. The recorded action is evidence used to confront it. If that distinction is unfamiliar, continue with the definitions below.

03

Key definitions and concepts

  • Behavioral economics. Uses psychological insights to change particular assumptions about economic choices while retaining precise, falsifiable predictions.
  • Experimental economics. A method of generating evidence, often by controlling information, incentives, and context to test explanations.
  • Behavioral mechanism. The proposed reason behind a choice—for example, overlooking available information.
  • Falsifiable prediction. A prediction that evidence could show to be wrong; it specifies an observable contrast and the conditions under which it should hold.
  • Correlation. An association between variables. By itself, it does not establish that one causes the other.
  • Counterfactual. What would happen under an alternative situation—for example, what someone would have done without an intervention.
  • Selfish preferences. Preferences that make utility depend only on a person’s own payoff; the stated benchmark excludes reputation-building.
  • Exogenous preferences. Motivations treated as largely fixed traits, unresponsive to personal experiences or environmental factors.
04

How behavioral models explain choices

4.1 Five ways assumptions can change

Behavioral economics uses psychological insights to adapt traditional economic assumptions, explain and predict choices, and inform policy. It is also called psychology and economics or psychological economics. The word “behavioral” identifies the use of cognitive and social psychology to improve assumptions about decision-making; other branches of economics also study behavior.

Five kinds of changes concern different parts of a decision. Distinguishing them gives us more precise explanations than a general claim that people are “irrational.” Their formal mathematical models come in later lessons.

What changes What part of a choice it brings into view
Broader motivations: fairness, social preferences, group identity, social norms A person's motivation can include the treatment of others or expectations associated with a group, alongside their own material outcome.
Imperfect self-control Carrying out a plan can itself be difficult; an intended choice and a later action need not coincide.
Imperfect attention Information can be available without receiving the decision-maker's attention.
Inaccurate beliefs The decision-maker's view of the situation can differ from the relevant facts.
Preferences may not be fixed The motivations used to explain choices need not be treated as unchanging.

For example, a fairness explanation changes motivations, while an inaccurate-beliefs explanation changes how someone understands the situation. Which mechanism we propose affects the prediction we make and the policy implication we might eventually draw. The categories organize possible explanations; they do not establish empirical results by themselves.

4.2 Whose payoff matters—and where preferences come from

Two traditional assumptions concern different aspects of preferences:

  • Selfish preferences: utility depends only on a person’s own payoff. In the benchmark without reputation-building, another person’s treatment or fairness does not enter utility.
  • Exogenous preferences: motivations are treated as largely fixed traits, unresponsive to personal experiences or environmental factors.

The distinction is between whose payoff matters and where preferences come from. Including another person’s payoff changes the content of utility. Allowing preferences to respond to experience changes how we explain their origins.

Payoff weights and behavior-dependent reciprocity develop the first issue: how concern for others affects choices. Social preferences can support cooperation and economic surplus, connecting them to social capital. They can also permit costly harm, as with spite or negative reciprocity.

Here, the focus is on those payoff concerns. A formal model of preference formation and estimates of how preferences evolve over a lifetime are outside this lesson.

05

From an explanation to a testable prediction

Changing an assumption is useful when it improves our explanation of choices and produces precise, falsifiable predictions—predictions that evidence could show to be wrong. A plausible story alone is insufficient.

For example, perfect rationality can be relaxed in a particular model without abandoning economic modeling or mathematics. The relevant question is whether that specific change gives a better account of choices; it does not imply that every traditional model fails.

5.1 Identify the mechanism

Suppose a person overlooks available reward information. The proposed mechanism is imperfect attention. This changes a different part of the explanation from fairness, inaccurate beliefs, or difficulty carrying out a plan.

5.2 Make the prediction testable

If attention affects the choice, making existing reward information more noticeable changes choices in otherwise comparable settings.

This is an illustrative conditional prediction, not a reported result. It states an observable contrast; evidence could show that the predicted difference does not occur under the stated conditions.

5.3 Connect theory, evidence, and application

Use the conventional microeconomic framework as a starting point, then connect three parts of the reasoning:

  • Theory or intuition: propose a mechanism and derive a prediction.
  • Evidence: test the prediction, often using controlled experiments.
  • Application: ask what supported findings would imply for a practical problem, such as rewarding employees, tackling poverty, or discrimination.

Example: rewarding employees. Begin with a proposed motivation, predict how behavior would differ under alternative incentives, and compare that prediction with evidence. Any recommendation about rewards depends on what the evidence supports.

This is a hypothetical example. It reports no actual experiment, wage effect, or preferred policy.

06

What evidence can tell us

6.1 An explanation and a method answer different questions

Behavioral economics concerns the assumptions and explanations used to understand choices. Experimental economics concerns how evidence is generated to test explanations. Calling a study experimental identifies its method; it does not, by itself, identify the behavioral assumptions being tested.

6.2 The missing comparison

Observing naturally occurring economic processes lets us study them in their undisturbed setting. But when two variables move together, that association alone does not tell us whether changing one would change the other.

A counterfactual asks what would happen under an alternative situation, including one that did not occur in the observed process. That alternative outcome is the missing comparison in many causal claims.

A before-and-after sequence has the same difficulty: what would have happened without the intervention? Temporal order alone does not supply that comparison.

6.3 How experimental control helps

An experiment can control information, incentives, and context to investigate counterfactual situations and estimate causal effects. Such control helps us get closer to the effect of changing a particular factor.

When evaluating evidence, ask which behavior the model explains, which assumption it changes, and how the comparison tests the proposed explanation. An association may be informative without establishing the causal effect needed for a policy argument.

Experimental control does not automatically solve every problem of causal interpretation. A causal conclusion still depends on the research design. This introductory comparison does not supply a complete identification strategy or estimator.

6.4 Read the correlation-and-causation cartoon

In the cartoon, a person reports changing their belief after a statistics class. Another person treats that sequence as evidence that the class caused the change; the reply leaves the inference uncertain.

The sequence is compatible with that explanation, but it provides no comparison showing what the person would have believed without the class. The cartoon illustrates the reasoning problem; it is not a research result.

07

Worked example: an employee reward system

Hypothetical example · an employee reward system

An employee-reward model adds concern about coworkers’ treatment. A researcher can compare incentive contexts; rewards and output are also naturally correlated.

7.1 Identify the mechanism

The amendment is broader motivation: the employee cares about coworkers’ treatment alongside their own material outcome. The other introductory directions are self-control, attention, beliefs, and preference stability.

7.2 State a conditional prediction

If coworker treatment matters, choices may differ across otherwise comparable incentive contexts. This is a testable proposal, not an observed effect.

7.3 Separate the explanation from the evidence

Concern about coworkers is the behavioral explanation. A comparison of controlled contexts describes experimental economics. The two can be used together.

7.4 Keep the conclusion within the evidence

Correlation alone cannot establish the effect of rewards: the alternative outcome under a different reward system is absent. Theory proposes the mechanism, evidence tests the prediction, and any employee-reward application follows conditionally. This example establishes no actual reward effect or preferred policy.

Check your understanding

Behavioral economics broadens assumptions about motivations, self-control, attention, beliefs, and preferences while retaining testable predictions. Experimental economics is a frequently used method for confronting those predictions with evidence and investigating causal effects through control of the research environment.

Keep the two questions separate: what explains a choice? And how can evidence test that explanation? Detailed social-preference models and later applications come after this introduction.

03

Key definitions and concepts

  • Marginal incentive: The change in expected pay from an additional unit of effort.
  • Fixed wage: The effort-independent payment in the supplied benchmark.
  • Social utility derivative: The change in social utility as effort changes, not merely the level of a wage gap.
04

Effort, contracts and social motivations

Why might a fixed wage change effort even when it does not change the monetary reward for an extra unit of work? Derive the self-regarding incentive benchmark, then changes the worker's utility. This separates an incentive effect from a social response to the employer or co-workers.

The map follows the lecture's model and its transition to field evidence. The core reasoning is marginal: a benefit that changes the level of utility but not its slope with respect to effort need not change effort.

4.1 The self-regarding principal-agent benchmark

The principal employs an agent whose effort \(e\) is not directly observable. Output is \(f(e)+\varepsilon\), with \(f'(e)>0\), \(f''(e)<0\) and mean-zero noise. Effort costs \(d(e)\), where \(d'(e)>0\) and \(d''(e)>0\). More effort raises expected output at a decreasing rate while its marginal cost rises. The simplified expected-pay contract is

\[y(e)=w+b f(e),\]

where \(w\) is fixed pay and \(b\) is the worker's output share. In the risk-neutral expected-pay representation, the noise has zero expected monetary contribution. If the actual contract pays on noisy observed output, a risk-averse worker would need a separate treatment of risk; that is outside the displayed benchmark.

The selfish worker chooses

\[e^*(w,b)\in\arg\max_e\{w+b f(e)-d(e)\}.\]

At an interior differentiable optimum,

\[b f'(e^*)=d'(e^*).\]

An extra unit of effort produces \(f'(e)\) expected output, of which the worker receives \(b f'(e)\); the worker compares that reward with \(d'(e)\).

Fixed pay \(w\) vanishes from this derivative. Under the stated concavity/convexity conditions the utility is concave for a nonnegative output share.

If effort must be nonnegative, a corner is possible: for \(b=0\), utility falls with effort and the optimum is the lowest feasible effort, written \(e=0\) in the lecture. The equality condition does not apply at that corner.

A higher fixed wage raises the level of selfish utility but supplies no extra marginal monetary reward for effort in this particular model.

The principal anticipates the agent's choice and writes

\[\max_{w,b}\{(1-b)f(e^*(w,b))-w\}.\]

This is expected output retained by the principal less fixed pay. The displayed objective does not supply participation constraints, outside options, permissible wages or a full risk-sharing problem, so it should not be used to solve an unrestricted global optimum for \(w\). Its role here is to show why the principal cares about the agent's effort response.

4.2 Adding a marginal social motive

The lecture adds a social component,

\[U_i=w+b f(e_i)-d(e_i)+S(e_i),\]

giving the interior condition

\[b f'(e_i)+\frac{\partial S}{\partial e_i}=d'(e_i).\]

The new term measures the utility benefit or cost of how extra effort affects socially valued outcomes. If it is positive, effort can be worthwhile even with little direct marginal pay.

This is a conditional model mechanism, not a claim that every gift raises effort. For a fixed wage to change the optimum, it must change a relevant marginal social motive, such as perceived kindness toward the employer, rather than simply add a constant to \(S\).

For altruism, the slide writes \(S_i=\sigma_{ij}U_j(e_i)\) with a positive weight on the other person's utility. If the employer benefits from extra effort, then \(\partial S_i/\partial e_i=\sigma_{ij}U_j'(e_i)\) can be positive.

If extra effort instead harms a cared-for peer, the same positive concern produces a negative social derivative and can lower effort. For reciprocity, kind treatment can make helping the employer attractive, while unkind treatment can reduce that willingness or create a motive to harm.

This depends on the treatment and its perceived intentions, not only on final wage levels.

For inequality aversion, the source gives the schematic \(S_i=\sigma_{ij}|y_i-y_j|\) with \(\sigma_{ij}<0\). A payoff gap lowers utility, but the effect on effort depends on how effort changes the two payoffs.

The slide's intuition is that a worker with an outcome above the norm may reduce effort, while one below it may increase effort. When own payoff rises with effort and peer payoff is fixed, the absolute gap rises with effort above the peer, so the negative coefficient creates a negative social derivative;

below the peer the gap falls with effort, giving a positive social derivative. More generally, the sign depends on how effort changes the payoff gap.

That intuition requires a channel from effort into payoffs or socially interpreted contributions. If both own and peer monetary wages are literally fixed and independent of effort, the displayed absolute wage gap is constant: this term alone has zero effort derivative and cannot generate that response.

The comparison is also nondifferentiable at equal pay. Treat the formula as a simplified motivation, not a general proof of every relative-pay effect.

The Week 1 note gives the fuller payoff-weight distinctions. Models of Social Preferences

4.3 Wage rigidity and the gift-exchange hypothesis

The opening supply-demand picture draws a wage above the market-clearing intersection, so labour supplied exceeds labour demanded. The horizontal gap is unemployment in that illustration.

It motivates the question of why firms might sustain wages above a competitive benchmark; the drawing does not establish the cause of observed unemployment.

Akerlof's gift-exchange idea is that firms offer favourable wages and workers reciprocate with effort above the minimum. Such responses can make high wages privately worthwhile under some conditions.

Read the wage, employment and excess-supply gap together. The later field studies test particular wage/effort responses, rather than proving a complete macroeconomic explanation from this stylized graph. The Sli.do prompt on the preceding lecture pages records a question, not observed class poll results.

The principal-Derivation and the distinction between marginal incentives and social motives are core because they generate predictions. The field applications then ask whether those predictions survive real effort, time and context. No slide or these notes establish that any specific derivation will appear in an exam.

Worked example

Prompt: Use a hypothetical risk-neutral expected-pay model on \(0\leq e\leq 9\): \(f(e)=10e-e^2/2\), \(d(e)=2e+e^2/2\), contract \(y=w+b f(e)\) with \(w=10\) and \(b=0.5\). Derive the selfish marginal condition and optimum, checking concavity and corners.

Does increasing only \(w\) by 5 change effort? Compare a fixed-pay contract \(b=0\) without social utility.

Now add the explicitly supplied \(S(e)=3e\) and solve effort. Explain how altruistic concern can increase or reduce the social derivative, why a literally fixed own/peer wage gap alone cannot affect effort, and what is unresolved at equality.

State the principal’s expected objective and why an unrestricted optimum for its wage cannot be solved from these notes alone.

Solution: \(f^{\prime}(e)=10-e>0\) on the supplied domain and \(f^{\prime\prime}=-1\); \(d^{\prime}(e)=2+e>0\) and \(d^{\prime\prime}=1\). Selfish derivative is \(b(10-e)-(2+e)\), second derivative \(-(b+1)<0\), and the unconstrained solution is \((0.5(10)-2)/(0.5+1)=2.00000000\) here, inside the bounds.

Clipping/checking endpoints handles a corner. Fixed \(w\) disappears from the derivative, so a 5-unit wage increase does not change effort in this benchmark.

At \(b=0\) and no social term the objective decreases with effort, giving the lowest feasible effort 0. With \(S^{\prime}(e)=3\), solve \(b(10-e)+3=2+e\), yielding \(4.00000000\), again feasible.

Positive altruistic weight times the other person’s effort-induced utility derivative can be positive when helping them or negative when effort harms them; reciprocity depends on treatment/intentions.

A negative coefficient on \(|y_i-y_j|\) has zero effort derivative if both payoffs are literally effort-independent. With an effort-varying gap its derivative may differ above/below the peer;

the absolute value is nondifferentiable at equality. The principal anticipates effort and maximizes \((1-b)f(e^*(w,b))-w\), but participation/outside-option/feasible-wage/risk-sharing constraints are absent, so no unrestricted global wage optimum or complete macro-unemployment explanation is inferred.

6.4 Another chance to practise

A worker receives two literally effort-independent fixed wages and dislikes their absolute gap.

Solve the following:

  1. Does that gap term alone change the effort FOC?

A local draft for your own study.

Reveal explanation

No. The gap and its utility penalty are constant in effort, so their derivative is zero. An effort-induced payoff/contribution or social-meaning channel is needed; at equal variable payoffs the absolute value is nondifferentiable.

7.1 Question 1

For \(f(e)=10e-e^2/2\), \(d(e)=2e+e^2/2\) and \(b=0.5\) on[0,9], what is the selfish interior effort?

Reveal explanation

Solve \(0.5(10-e)=2+e\); the concave optimum is interior at 2.

7.2 Question 2

What happens to effort if only fixed wage w rises by 5 in this benchmark?

Reveal explanation

An effort-independent wage disappears from the first-order condition under the maintained benchmark.

7.3 Question 3

Adding \(S(e)=3e\) changes the same interior effort to what value?

Reveal explanation

Solve \(0.5(10-e)+3=2+e\) to get 4, within the stated bounds.

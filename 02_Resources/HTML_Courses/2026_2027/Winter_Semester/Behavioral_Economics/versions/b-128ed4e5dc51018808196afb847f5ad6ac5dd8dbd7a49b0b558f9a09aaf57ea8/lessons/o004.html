Introduction

In this lesson, distinguish reciprocity, payoff concerns and efficiency.

2.1 What you’ll learn

  • Explain \(q=1\) kindness/\(q=-1\) misbehavior, \(\theta >0\), derive effective weights and \(2\theta\) change, and compute treatment-dependent utilities.
  • State negative response weight iff \(\theta >\rho\); distinguish treatment from unconditional altruism and payoff inequality; fixed allocation utility need not increase after kindness.

03

Key definitions and concepts

  • Reciprocity: Concern for another person depends on how that person behaved.
  • Unconditional payoff concern: The weight on another person’s payoff need not depend on their behavior.
  • Efficiency: The total monetary payoff, distinct from one person’s utility.

3.1 Notation and distinctions

No visual asset is necessary for this objective; use the explicit equations, labels and reasoning prepared here.

04

Reciprocity responds to prior treatment

Reciprocity means kindness toward someone who acted kindly and hostility toward someone who acted unkindly. The source adds \(q=1\) for person 2's kind action and \(q=-1\) for misbehavior, with sensitivity \(\theta>0\):

\[U_1(x_1,x_2)=(\rho+\theta q)x_2+(1-\rho-\theta q)x_1.\]

Define the effective social weight \(w(q)=\rho+\theta q\). A kind action gives \(w(1)=\rho+\theta\);

misbehavior gives \(w(-1)=\rho-\theta\). Thus treatment changes the weight on the other person's payoff by \(2\theta\) between these two states.

A negative response weight follows if \(\theta>\rho\); \(\theta>0\) alone does not ensure that the total weight becomes negative. These comparisons unpack the supplied equation.

Reciprocity therefore differs from unconditional altruism and from payoff inequality alone. Two identical monetary allocations can have different utility if prior treatment changes \(q\).

Define the two kindness states but supplies no detailed rule for measuring perceived intentions or estimating \(\theta\). The displayed equation is a simple illustration, not a full solution of every strategic game or a replacement of the earlier two-regime function in all contexts.

05

Efficiency-seeking and the welfare connection

The lecture names efficiency-seeking alongside the other social preferences and lists it as a possible reason for trustor transfers. Its relevant intuition is concern for the total monetary surplus that cooperation can create. The presentation does not give a separate complete efficiency-seeking utility function or identify it from one observed transfer.

Hypothetical illustration using the displayed model: \(\rho=\sigma=1/2\) yields \(U_1=(x_1+x_2)/2\), so maximizing this particular utility is equivalent to maximizing total monetary payoff. This illustrates how the existing payoff weights can value a larger total;

it is not a claim that the lecture estimates those parameters or that all efficiency-seeking preferences take this exact form. Concern about totals, distribution and kind treatment can produce similar choices in some games, making the experimental comparison important.

Trust and cooperation games develops the distinction between splitting a fixed pie and expanding monetary surplus. Social preferences can encourage the latter, while beliefs and incentives still determine whether cooperation occurs.

The central result of these models is a set of distinct mechanisms—positive payoff weights, negative payoff weights, disparity penalties and treatment-dependent weights—not a single universal “unselfish” preference.

06

Reciprocity needs an expectation about the other player

The lecture's second explanation is positive reciprocity combined with the belief that the other person will cooperate. It cites Dawes and Thaler (1988) beside the observation that cooperation occurs even with a finite number of interactions, sometimes only once;

the uploaded slide does not supply the underlying study design, sample, or numerical cooperation rate. Positive reciprocity means wanting to respond kindly to perceived kindness.

In a simultaneous game there is no observed current action to reciprocate at the moment of choice, so an expectation about the other's cooperation is part of this proposed explanation. The lecture does not specify a separate payoff formula or a quantitative belief threshold for this mechanism.

This differs from the trust game, where the trustee observes the initial transfer before returning money. It also differs from the unconditional warm-glow premium: a desire to reward kindness depends on how the other player's behavior is perceived or expected. The reciprocity model makes this dependence explicit.

07

Connect the reasoning

  1. In the displayed reciprocity equation define \(w(q)=\rho+\theta q\): utility is \(w(q)x_2+[1-w(q)]x_1\). Kindness gives \(w_+=\rho+\theta\), misbehavior \(w_-=\rho-\theta\), so \(w_+-w_-=2\theta>0\).
  2. Negative total weight after misbehavior requires \(\rho-\theta<0\), equivalently \(\theta>\rho\). A positive \(\theta\) alone cannot establish hostility; baseline \(\rho\) can be larger. At \(\theta =\rho\) the negative-treatment weight is zero.
  3. Identical allocations can have different utilities because \(q\) changes; this differs from unconditional altruism and inequality-only weighting. Do not say kindness raises utility for every fixed allocation: \(U_+-U_-=2\theta(x_2-x_1)\), so the sign depends on relative monetary payoffs. This is direct unpacking of the supplied equation, not an estimated treatment effect.
  4. As the notes’ labelled illustration, set both earlier payoff weights to \(1/2\): \(U=(x_1+x_2)/2\). Ranking this particular utility is equivalent to ranking the total. This supplies an illustration of efficiency concern within the existing function, not a complete separately estimated efficiency-seeking model or evidence that all efficiency preferences use these weights.
  5. Sequential trust lets a trustee observe the prior transfer; simultaneous cooperation requires a belief about current kindness before either action is seen. The lecture names positive reciprocity plus belief in cooperation but gives no separate quantitative simultaneous mechanism, measured intention rule, study rate or belief threshold. A single transfer cannot isolate the motivation.

Worked example

8.1 — Treatment shifts weight

Setup: Hypothetical \(\rho =1/5,\theta =3/10\), allocation \((40,20)\); compare \(q\) states and efficiency illustration \((24,24)\) versus \((30,26)\).

Worked reasoning and result: Kind weight \(.5\),unkind \(-.1\),change \(.6\); \(U_{\mathrm{kind}}=.5(20)+.5(40)=30\), \(U_{\mathrm{unkind}}=-.1(20)+1.1(40)=42\). Negative after misbehavior since \(\theta >\rho\);

kindness raises other-weight but lowers this fixed allocation’s utility because \(\text{own}>\text{other}\). Fixed altruism/inequality do not respond to \(q\).

Efficiency illustration utilities \(24\) and \(28\), prefers larger total \(56\). It is not a separate efficiency model or motive identification;

simultaneous reciprocity requires expectations whereas trustee sees transfer, no quantitative mechanism supplied.

Checks and interpretation: Verify labels, active conditions, dimensions/signs where applicable and every source limitation before inferring a choice.

Check your reasoning: an offline quiz

Choose one answer per question. Check individual answers or check the whole quiz. Each option includes feedback on the reasoning, and everything works offline.

10.1 Question 1

In the prepared model, baseline weight 0.2 and reciprocity shift 0.3 give which kind/unkind other-payoff weights?

Reveal explanation

The kind state adds 0.3 to 0.2; the unkind state subtracts it.

10.2 Question 2

Why can observing the same transfer fail to identify reciprocity?

Reveal explanation

Payoff concern, inequality concern and reciprocity can overlap in their observed choices.

10.3 Question 3

At own 40 and other 20, how does increasing the other-payoff weight from −0.1 to 0.5 affect utility in the weighted-payoff example?

Reveal explanation

At weightw, utility isw·20+(1−w)40; a larger weight on the lower payoff reduces this fixed allocation’s utility.

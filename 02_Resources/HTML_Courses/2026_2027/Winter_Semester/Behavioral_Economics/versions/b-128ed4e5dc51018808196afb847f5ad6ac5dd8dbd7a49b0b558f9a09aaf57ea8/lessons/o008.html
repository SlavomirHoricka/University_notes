02

Introduction

In this lesson, use course requirements and recognize exam-scope boundaries.

2.1 What you’ll learn

  • Reconstruct supplied course identity, teaching/preparation/prerequisite and appointment information with its documentary scope.
  • Apply exam format, scoring, tool restrictions and integrity declaration accurately.
  • Distinguish four offered slots from three attempts; retrieve dates/times/room, booking limits, printed grade bands and absent rounding rule.
  • Separate completed expanded Week 1 learning from later syllabus/sample exam orientation, absent answer keys/polls and conditional misperceptions; avoid claiming a personal deadline or full exam readiness.

2.2 Before you begin

  • No earlier lesson in this course is required. Review the definitions below before the worked example.

Key definitions and concepts

  • Exam slot: A listed scheduling option, distinct from the permitted number of attempts.
  • Attempt limit: The maximum number of exam attempts in the supplied course information.
  • Prepared scope: The checked material actually taught here, distinct from every later exam topic.

04

Course organization and exam

This note records the uploaded 2026 course information for Behavioral Economics (JEB147) and the requirements introduced in Week 1. It is a record of the supplied documents, not a live check of SIS. The course folder keeps its established descriptive name.

Return to . The conceptual introduction is in Behavioral and experimental economics.

4.1 Teaching and preparation

Michal Bauer and Julie Chytilová teach the course. Teaching is in person. The introduction invites questions during lectures and at their end, and describes a sequence of theory or intuition, experimental evidence, and applications. Slides are posted in SIS and reading the papers is strongly encouraged. Microeconomics I (JEB104) is the prerequisite.

The syllabus lists Bauer in office 402 with Friday office hours 11:00–11:45, and Chytilová in office 410 with Friday office hours 10:30–11:45; it asks students to email first. The introductory slides say Bauer's appointments can be arranged by email. These are the supplied descriptions, without an assumption about current availability.

4.2 Assessment format and dates

The grade is based on the written final test. The exam-information document sets a maximum of 100 points and an 80-minute duration.

It includes multiple-choice questions with exactly one correct answer and open-ended text answers. Incorrect answers receive no negative points.

The exam is closed book: the supplied rules prohibit supporting materials, devices, calculators, and AI tools, while allowing blank paper and pens. Students must agree to the academic-integrity declaration for their test to be evaluated;

refusal is graded F.

These dates agree across the syllabus, introduction, and uploaded SIS export. Four dates are offered, but the exam-information document permits at most three attempts. It sets registration at least 48 hours before the exam and deregistration at least 24 hours before it.

The documents print the following grading ranges: A 90.1–100, B 80.1–90, C 70.1–80, D 60.1–70, E 50.1–60, F 0–50. They do not explain rounding or how finer fractional scores between the printed ranges would be assigned; the note preserves the ranges without adding a rule.

4.3 Boundary of the Week 1 notes

Topic 1 asks for the definition of behavioral economics, its difference from experimental economics, and the course overview and requirements. The syllabus's later topics and bibliographies are a map of the rest of the course.

The SIS export marks the misperceptions topic “if time allows,” whereas the syllabus lists it without that qualification; neither statement changes the selected Week 1 material.

No later-topic readings have been ingested in this pass.

The sample test includes inequality aversion, the trust game, statistical discrimination, the ultimatum game, comparisons of discrimination models, and a question about intergroup contact referring to Corno et al. (2022). These are examples of the final exam's scope and question formats.

Their substantive answers require later-topic material and are deliberately excluded from this Week 1 introduction. The uploaded example document supplies questions and answer options, not an answer key.

4.4 Added social-preferences presentation and current Week 1 scope

The Week_1 materials now also contain the complete Social Preferences presentation. Its allocation games, payoff-weight models, inequality aversion, reciprocity, trust game and prisoner's dilemma are covered in Ultimatum and dictator games, Models of social preferences, and Trust and cooperation games.

The boundary section above records the original introductory pass and the contents of the exam-information source; it does not exclude this added presentation from the expanded collection.

The sample-test questions still have no supplied answer key. Some underlying concepts now have separate teaching notes, but the statistical-discrimination and intergroup-contact readings are not supplied by this added deck. Its poll pages export only questions, not class responses or results.

Connect the reasoning

  1. Identify the course and its conventional prerequisite, then reproduce the theory–evidence–application study sequence and supplied appointment information. The documents describe schedules, without a live availability check.
  2. Distinguish assessment format from preparation tools. Supported practice can use notes, but independent checks mirror the supplied closed-book restriction with only blank paper and pens. Exactly one correct multiple-choice option differs from multiple-response selection; open text requires reasoning. No negative points does not invent a partial-credit policy. Refusing the integrity declaration prevents evaluation and is F.
  3. Read the dates as offered slots. The exam lasts 80 minutes, maximum 100 points, at most three attempts despite four slots; registration and deregistration have separate 48/24-hour limits. Printed grade ranges leave finer fractions and rounding unspecified. Do not fill those gaps or schedule an attempt.
  4. Separate the original introduction from the expanded social-preferences collection. Later syllabus/sample topics identify future scope; they do not supply an answer key, formal later models or discrimination/intergroup-contact readings. Current social-preference concepts are supported without making every sample question ready. Misperceptions has a SIS condition absent from the syllabus. No exported poll responses exist.
  5. The historical P001 case has an unknown personal deadline (null) and partial whole-semester coverage. Any chosen exam/timing constraint needs explicit user evidence; later academic material goes to ingest before curriculum expansion.

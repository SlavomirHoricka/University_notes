Introduction

In this lesson, convert futures quotations into payoff and conditional loss bounds.

2.1 What you’ll learn

  • Convert cents and fractional quotations
  • Calculate tick values and signed long/short cumulative payoff with total contract exposure
  • Justify maximum-loss claims from explicit price bounds.
  • Interpret zero crossing and slope as trading \(\mathrm{P/L}\) rather than account balance. Assume fixed \(N\) and \(q\) and ignore settlement-flow interest; a maturity use of spot additionally assumes \(F_T=S_T\).

03

Key definitions and concepts

  • Contract multiplier: Underlying units or money per quoted point for one contract.
  • Tick value: Minimum quoted price movement multiplied by the contract multiplier.
  • Trading profit/loss: Signed change in contract value, distinct from the margin-account balance.
04

Standardized futures specifications

A futures contract is a standardized agreement for a future exchange, traded on a specialized exchange. The trader accepts the published terms and chooses whether to buy or sell at the available futures price. Standardization makes contracts comparable and allows an opposite trade to unwind a position. Consider this ease of offsetting as an important source of liquidity.

A futures contract is a derivative because its price is derived from the underlying asset's price. The specifications identify the underlying asset, contract size, delivery month, quotation and minimum price movement, and delivery arrangements.

An underlying asset may be a commodity, an index, an equity, a currency or an interest-rate-related instrument. For commodities, the grade of quality matters because goods of different quality need not have the same price.

Delivery location also matters when transport costs are significant; alternative locations can require price adjustments.

The contract multiplier converts a quoted price movement into a monetary gain or loss. If a price is quoted in dollars per barrel and the contract covers 1,000 barrels, a $0.01 rise per barrel changes the contract's monetary value by $10. If an index contract pays $50 per index point and its minimum movement is 0.25 points, one tick is $12.50. In both cases,

\[\text{tick value}=\text{tick size}\times\text{contract multiplier}.\]

The units must cancel correctly: dollars per barrel times barrels gives dollars; index points times dollars per point gives dollars. For \(N\) contracts, multiply the monetary change by \(N\). These are the seminar's examples, rather than claims that every oil or index contract uses those specifications.

The seminar's fractional quotation example is \(99+21.75/32=99.6796875\), or approximately 99.68, for the written quote \(99\text{-}21\tfrac34\). Its text first discusses Treasury bonds and then labels the example “US T-Bill”; the label is internally inconsistent, so the verified learning point here is the arithmetic of the fractional quote.

The seminar describes usual maturities as ranging from one month to two years. This is its introductory description; a particular contract's published terms determine its available maturities.

Delivery month is part of the contract's identity: a June contract and a September contract are different obligations. The lecture and seminar describe March, June, September and December as usual financial-contract months, and the seminar says commodity contracts may be available monthly.

These are the supplied introductory descriptions; the specified contract calendar remains necessary for an actual contract.

A contract that is too large can exclude smaller traders, while one that is too small increases the number of transactions needed for a desired exposure.

05

Daily profit and loss, with units

Let \(F_0\) be the opening futures price, \(F_t\) the settlement price at the end of day t, \(N\) the number of contracts and \(q\) the multiplier converting one quoted price unit into money per contract. With a constant position size and multiplier,

\[G_t^{\mathrm{long}}=Nq(F_t-F_{t-1}),\qquad G_t^{\mathrm{short}}=Nq(F_{t-1}-F_t)=-G_t^{\mathrm{long}}.\]

A price rise credits the long and debits the short; a fall reverses these transfers. The lecture writes the price difference for a normalized unit; \(Nq\) restores the contract units used by the seminar's tick examples. For a commodity quoted in dollars per pound, \(q\) is pounds per contract. For a contract quoted directly in dollars per contract, \(q=1\).

Adding the daily transfers cancels every intermediate price:

\[\sum_{t=1}^{k}G_t^{\mathrm{long}} =Nq\big[(F_1-F_0)+(F_2-F_1)+\cdots +(F_k-F_{k-1})\big] =Nq(F_k-F_0).\]

The short obtains the opposite amount. This telescoping sum explains why the total trading result equals the closing minus opening price, even though it is paid in installments. The account can require cash along the way even when the final cumulative result is favorable. The source's reasoning ignores interest earned or paid on the timing of settlement cash flows.

06

Payoff and the size of possible losses

For a position held to maturity with \(F_T=S_T\), cumulative trading results are

\[\Pi_T^{\mathrm{long}}=Nq(S_T-F_0),\qquad \Pi_T^{\mathrm{short}}=Nq(F_0-S_T).\]

They have equal magnitude and opposite signs. A long benefits from a higher final underlying price; a short benefits from a lower one. The source diagram crosses zero at \(S_T=F_0\) and shows linear results on either side. Margin does not truncate either line.

The horizontal axis is the maturity spot price \(S_T\). The long line slopes upward and the short line downward; their common break-even point is the opening futures price \(F_0\). The vertical direction represents gain/loss, not the margin account balance.

A claimed maximum long loss needs a lower bound on the price. If the modeled final price cannot go below zero, the maximum loss is \(NqF_0\) at zero.

If the problem imposes no lower bound, that particular finite maximum does not follow from the payoff formula. With no upper price bound, a short's theoretical loss has no finite maximum.

These are algebraic consequences of the source payoff, and the required assumptions are made explicit in the copper exercise.

07

Problem 3: copper and maximum loss

One long November contract covers 25,000 pounds at $0.78 per pound. Its normalized cumulative result at a closing/settlement price \(F\) is

\[\Pi^{\mathrm{long}}=25000(F-0.78).\]

If one additionally assumes \(F\) cannot be negative, the largest loss is \(25000\times0.78=\$19,500\), reached at \(F=0\). The question itself does not state a lower price bound, so \(19,500\) is a conditional result rather than a bound implied by futures margin.

If the model admits arbitrarily negative \(F\), the long formula has no finite loss bound. For the short, \(\Pi^{\mathrm{short}}=25000(0.78-F)\): with no upper price bound, an arbitrarily high \(F\) gives an arbitrarily large loss.

The initial margin is never a maximum-loss guarantee.

09

Connect the reasoning

  1. Convert every quote to the units matched by \(q\). Dollars per pound times pounds gives dollars.
  2. A long earns \(Nq(F_{\mathrm{close}}-F_{\mathrm{open}})\); a short earns \(Nq(F_{\mathrm{open}}-F_{\mathrm{close}})\). Opposite sides sum to zero for the same inputs.
  3. Daily differences telescope, so cumulative payoff uses endpoints for a fixed position; at maturity replace closing \(F\) by \(S\) only under convergence. Break-even is \(\text{final price}=F_{\mathrm{open}}\); long slope +\(Nq\) and short −\(Nq\).
  4. Margin is the trader's deposit, not a payoff ceiling or asset price. With \(F\ge 0\), a long's maximum loss is \(NqF_{\mathrm{open}}\) at zero. If no lower bound exists this finite maximum is not established. With no upper bound, short loss is unbounded. Price/position limits likewise do not truncate the payoff line.
  5. A changed number example is a parallel calculation, not proof of broad transfer. Keep given price-bound assumptions visible in the answer.
10

Worked example

Source copper case: one long, \(q=25,000\) pounds, opening $0.78/lb. At \(F=\$0.70/\text{lb}\), hypothetical closing price, \(\mathrm{P/L}=25,000(0.70-0.78)=-\$2,000\);

short opposite +$2,000. If \(F\ge 0\), maximum long loss \(=\$19,500\) at zero, independent of margin.

If negative prices are unconstrained no finite bound follows; short with no upper price bound has no finite maximum loss.

One $0.01/lb tick is $250 for this specified hypothetical tick. Source index example \(0.25\text{ points}\times\$50/\text{point}=\$12.50\) per contract.

Source film calculation: \(450,000\) pounds short at \(1.42\) and offset at \(0.29\) gains \(450,000\times 1.13=\$508,500\), equal to \(30\text{ contracts}\times15,000\text{ pounds}\); this supplies no account path or legal explanation.

Write your reasoning before revealing an explanation. Begin with support, then apply the ideas to another situation.

11.1 Written exercise 1

A hypothetical long holds two \(1,000\)-unit contracts, opens at \(150\) cents/unit and closes at \(155\) cents.

Solve the following:

  1. Fill: \(F_{\mathrm{open}}=\underline{\hspace{1.5em}}\) dollars, \(\text{change}=\underline{\hspace{1.5em}}\) dollars, \(\text{exposure}=\underline{\hspace{1.5em}}\) units, \(\text{long result}=\underline{\hspace{1.5em}}\), \(\text{short opposite}=\underline{\hspace{1.5em}}\).
  2. A tick is \(1\) cent;
  3. give position tick value.
  4. Convert \(98\text{–}16\) in thirty-seconds.
  5. State the maximum long loss if \(F\ge 0\) and whether a $100 margin caps it.
Show a hint

Pause before calculating or choosing an explanation:

  • What quantities, actors or outcomes are being compared? Write their roles, units and dates beside them.
  • Which condition decides whether the relationship applies? Check the relevant definition before using a formula or classifying the case.
  • What would have to remain fixed for your comparison to be meaningful? Work through one link in the reasoning at a time.
  • How could you check the direction, boundary case or alternative explanation before stating your conclusion?

A local draft for your own study.

Reveal explanation

Key: \(1.50\); \(+0.05\); \(2,000\); +$100; -$100; $20/tick. \(98+16/32=98.5\). Maximum loss $3,000, not $100; without lower bound no such finite bound, short unbounded absent upper bound. Require units/sign and bounded-loss reasoning.

Allowed help: reveal the named definitions, then at most two prepared hints in Misconceptions and correction.

11.2 Written exercise 2

Hypothetical three long contracts each cover 1,200 pounds.

Solve the following:

  1. Price opens 176 cents/lb and closes 168 cents/lb;
  2. tick 2 cents/lb.
  3. Give converted prices, long and opposite short \(\mathrm{P/L}\), position tick value, and break-even/slope direction.
  4. Decode 100–11½ in thirty-seconds.
  5. With \(F\ge 0\), find maximum long loss;
  6. without a lower bound or upper bound state what can be claimed for long and short.
  7. Does $225 margin cap either loss?

A local draft for your own study.

Reveal explanation

Explanation: \(1.76\to 1.68\) dollars/lb; exposure \(3\times 1,200=3,600\) lb.

Long \(3,600(1.68-1.76)=-\$288\); opposite short +$288.

Tick \(0.02\times 3,600=\$72\) per position tick. Break-even \(1.76\) dollars/lb;

long payoff rises and short payoff falls with final price. Fraction \(100+11.5/32=100.359375\);

the source label inconsistency does not identify an instrument. With \(F\ge 0\), maximum long loss is \(3,600\times 1.76=\$6,336\) at zero.

Without a lower price bound no finite long maximum is established; without an upper price bound a short has unbounded loss.

Margin is separate account funding and does not truncate payoff. All three components and their stated assumptions/units are required.

11.3 Written exercise 3

Hypothetical two short index contracts have $40 per point, opening 1800 and closing 1807, tick 0.5 points.

Solve the following:

  1. Calculate both sides' \(\mathrm{P/L}\) and total tick value with units;
  2. identify break-even and slope.
  3. Separately decode 103–10¼ in thirty-seconds and convert 65 cents to dollars.
  4. For one long 18,000-lb contract at 65 cents with \(F\ge 0\), give maximum loss;
  5. then remove the lower bound and discuss a short without an upper bound and why an initial deposit does not settle that question.

A local draft for your own study.

Reveal explanation

Explanation: Exposure \(2\times\$40=\$80/\text{point}\). Short \(80(1800-1807)=-\$560\);

opposite long +$560. Tick \(0.5\times 80=\$40\) per position tick.

Break-even \(1800\) points; short payoff slopes downward and long payoff upward.

Fraction \(103+10.25/32=103.3203125\); \(65\text{ cents}=\$0.65\). For the separate nonnegative-price long, maximum loss is \(18,000\times 0.65=\$11,700\) at zero.

Removing the lower price bound removes that finite bound; absence of an upper price bound makes short loss unbounded.

Initial deposit is separate funding and cannot bound the price-based payoff. All three components and their stated assumptions/units are required.

Check your reasoning: an offline quiz

Choose one answer per question. Check individual answers or check the whole quiz. Each option includes feedback on the reasoning, and everything works offline.

12.1 Question 1

One long covers 25,000 lb, opening at $0.78/lb and closing at $0.70/lb. What is trading P/L?

Reveal explanation

A long earns \(25,000(0.70-0.78)=-2,000\) dollars.

12.2 Question 2

For that long, what follows if prices are bounded below by zero?

Reveal explanation

At zero, the loss is 25,000×0.78. The finite bound relies on the stated lower bound.

12.3 Question 3

A tick is 0.25 index point with $50 per point. What is one-contract tick value?

Reveal explanation

Multiply the quoted point change by dollars per point, rather than divide or add.

Check your understanding

Avoid these mistakes: hundredfold cent error, reversed short sign, maximum loss equated to margin, fractional digits treated as decimals. Correction: cancel units first, choose winning direction before multiplying, and derive the bound by the allowed extreme final price.

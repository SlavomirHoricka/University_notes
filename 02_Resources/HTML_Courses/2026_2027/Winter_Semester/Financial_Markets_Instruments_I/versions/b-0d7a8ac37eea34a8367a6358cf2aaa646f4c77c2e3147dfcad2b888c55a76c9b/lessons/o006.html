Interpret basis and explain conditional convergence

Explanation, worked examples and practice

Useful first: Distinguish futures contract operation and its limits

01

Outcome and conditions

Given spot and two futures maturities, compute the course basis and calendar spread, identify the supplied lecture orderings, and explain convergence using both spot/futures delivery directions with execution assumptions. Distinguish short selling an asset from a short futures position, EDSP procedure from a universal formula, and endpoint convergence from smooth movement. No cost-of-carry pricing model is included.

03

Motivation, intuition, and definitions

Motivation: A spot–futures gap and a two-maturity gap answer different questions. \(S_t\) is contemporaneous spot; \(F_t^{X}\) futures for earlier \(X\), \(F_t^{Y}\) for later \(Y\). Course basis \(B^{X}=S-F^{X}\); calendar spread \(D^{(X,Y)}=F^{X}-F^{Y}\). Contango \(F^{X}>S\) gives negative \(B\); backwardation \(F^{X}<S\) gives positive \(B\). Lecture normal contango \(F^{Y}>F^{X}>S\); normal backwardation \(F^{Y}<F^{X}<S\). Opposite sign conventions exist; name one explicitly. Week \(1\) names expectations, carrying/opportunity costs and expected payments as drivers, without a pricing derivation.

04

Explanation steps

  1. Keep prices contemporaneous; compare spot against one maturity for \(B\) and futures against futures for D. Do not call D a basis.
  2. Substitute the declared signs. A negative \(B\) says futures above spot; a negative D says later above earlier. Labels follow those comparisons, not a forecast of rising spot.
  3. At delivery if \(F>S\), buy eligible asset spot for \(S\) and deliver using a short futures for \(F\), gross difference \(F-S\). If \(S>F\), obtain via long futures for \(F\) and sell spot for \(S\), gross \(S-F\). A borrowing-based spot short sale requires asset borrowing and return; short futures itself is a contractual sale.
  4. These executable positive differences drive theoretical \(F_T=S_T\)/\(B_T=0\) only under eligibility/matching delivery, feasible purchase/sale/borrowing and ignored costs. Nonzero costs or unavailable trades break the simple comparison; do not assert a free guaranteed trade in all markets.
  5. EDSP is contract-defined final settlement reference. Sources describe a closing procedure/selected-spot average but do not supply one universal computation. Convergence concerns each contract's own delivery endpoint; neither spot nor futures must move smoothly or monotonically. The source convergence asset illustrates curved paths.

Basis and calendar spread are different comparisons

For a delivery month \(X\), let \(S_t\) be the spot price at time t and \(F_t^X\) the futures price at that time for delivery in \(X\). This course uses

\[B_t^X=S_t-F_t^X.\]

The basis compares spot with one futures maturity. Contango means \(F_t^X>S_t\), so the basis is negative; backwardation means \(F_t^X<S_t\), so it is positive. Some markets use the opposite definition, \(F-S\); always state the convention before assigning signs. The seminar sometimes spells this quantity “Base,” but the lecture and handout use “basis.” L10(EssentialsOfFuturesTrading).pptx, slide 8 FI01(Futures Contracts).doc, Ch. I, LibreOffice-rendered p. 6 02_seminar.pdf, PDF p. 10

A calendar spread compares two futures prices. If \(X\) is the earlier delivery month and Y the later one, the lecture defines

\[D_t^{X,Y}=F_t^X-F_t^Y.\]

It also notes the opposite sign convention. The lecture's normal contango ordering is \(F_t^Y>F_t^X>S_t\), while its normal backwardation ordering is \(F_t^Y<F_t^X<S_t\). These are the meanings of those labels in the supplied lecture; do not substitute a different definition of “normal” without stating it. L10(EssentialsOfFuturesTrading).pptx, slide 8 FI01(Futures Contracts).doc, Ch. I, LibreOffice-rendered p. 7

The seminar lists market expectations, cost of carrying a position (including opportunity cost), and payments expected before delivery as reasons spot and futures prices can differ. Week 1 introduces these factors; the cost-of-carry pricing model in the later handout chapter is outside this ingestion scope. 02_seminar.pdf, PDF p. 10

Why the prices converge at delivery

The source's no-arbitrage argument compares executable spot purchase/sale with delivery under the matching futures contract. If \(F>S\) at delivery, buy the asset spot for \(S\) and deliver it through a short futures position for \(F\). If \(S>F\), buy through a long futures position for \(F\) and sell the asset spot for \(S\). Either case produces a positive difference before trading, delivery or borrowing costs. The handout's examples use \(F=100\), \(S=80\) or \(F=80\), \(S=100\), producing a difference of \(20\) per unit. L10(EssentialsOfFuturesTrading).pptx, slide 9 FI01(Futures Contracts).doc, Ch. I, LibreOffice-rendered p. 6

The theoretical conclusion is \(F_T=S_T\), hence \(B_T=0\). It presumes the compared asset is eligible for the contract and the required purchases, sales and deliveries can occur on the relevant terms; otherwise the cash-flow comparison is not established. In the second case, the handout also describes selling a borrowed security spot, taking futures delivery and returning it. Short selling of the asset involves borrowing and later returning that asset; it differs operationally from merely opening a short futures position. FI01(Futures Contracts).doc, Ch. I, LibreOffice-rendered p. 6–7

The Exchange Delivery Settlement Price, or EDSP, is the price used in final settlement. The handout calls it the closing price on the last trading day, determined by the clearing house through an exact procedure. The lecture gives an average of selected spot quotations as its introductory description and says its determination implements zero basis. These descriptions establish that final settlement follows a defined reference procedure; they do not supply a universal formula covering every contract type. FI01(Futures Contracts).doc, Ch. I, LibreOffice-rendered p. 6 L10(EssentialsOfFuturesTrading).pptx, slide 7, 9

The green line is spot and the red lines are futures for the earlier month \(X\) and later month Y. Each futures line meets spot at its own delivery date \(D^X\) or \(D^Y\). The curves need not be straight or monotonic: convergence concerns the endpoint, not a prediction that either price moves smoothly toward it. L10(EssentialsOfFuturesTrading).pptx, slide 9

06

Worked example

O006-W1 / item_version 1 / worked-example; source-supported example or explicitly hypothetical instantiation.

Hypothetical \(S=80\),\(F^{X}=90\),\(F^{Y}=95\). \(B^{X}=-10\),\(D=-5\), lecture normal contango \(95>90>80\). At \(X\) delivery, hypothetical \(F=100\)/\(S=80\) offers spot buy \(80\) plus futures short delivery \(100\), gross \(20\)/unit if terms permit. Reverse \(F=80\)/\(S=100\): obtain at \(80\) via long delivery and sell spot \(100\), gross \(20\); if spot sale precedes delivery, borrowing/return is needed. Ignoring costs, both discrepancies disappear in the matching no-arbitrage endpoint. EDSP implements final contract reference; do not infer a universal average or smooth price path.

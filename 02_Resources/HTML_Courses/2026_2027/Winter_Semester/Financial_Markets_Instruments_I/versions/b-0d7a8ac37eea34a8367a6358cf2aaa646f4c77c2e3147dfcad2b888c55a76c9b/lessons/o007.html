Week 01/Lesson 07

Derive matched purchase and sale hedge cash flows

Explanation, worked examples and practice

Useful first: Convert futures quotations into payoff and conditional loss bounds, Interpret basis and explain conditional convergence, Build daily margin accounts and reconcile trading result

01

Outcome and conditions

For matching fixed quantities, choose long purchase hedge or short sale hedge, derive and calculate the futures leg plus spot leg, report positive buyer expenditure, negative buyer signed cash flow and seller net receipt, and interpret residual basis and funding. Assume matching underlying/quantity/maturity, ignored transaction costs and interest on daily margin flows; a hedge of a different asset or quantity is outside this prepared scope.

03

Motivation, intuition, and definitions

Motivation: A losing futures leg can be part of a successful price hedge. \(X=Nq\) matched units; \(F_0\) opening, \(F_t\) closing, \(S_t\) spot at hedge transaction date; \(B=S_t-F_t\). A buyer opens long because a rise in later purchase cost should be offset by a futures gain. A seller opens short because a fall in sale receipt should be offset by a gain. Define \(E\) positive expenditure, \(\mathrm{CF}\) buyer signed cash flow with outflows negative, and \(R\) seller positive net receipt.

04

Explanation steps

  1. Buyer: futures gain \(X(F_t-F_0)\); spot purchase costs \(X S_t\). \(E=X S_t-X(F_t-F_0)=X[F_0+(S_t-F_t)]=X(F_0+B)\). \(\mathrm{CF}=-E\).
  2. Seller: spot revenue \(X S_t\) plus short gain \(X(F_0-F_t)\). \(R=X S_t+X(F_0-F_t)=X(F_0+B)\). The same expression has a different economic label; do not swap buyer expenditure and seller inflow signs.
  3. At matching maturity conditional convergence \(B_T=0\) gives \(E=R=X F_0\) and \(\mathrm{CF}=-X F_0\). Futures loss on a falling market is offset by cheaper purchase; futures loss on rising market by higher sale price for seller.
  4. Before maturity the closing basis is unknown at inception. The early hedge still operates but leaves buyer expenditure \(E=XF_0+XB_t\) or seller receipt \(R=XF_0+XB_t\), rather than a guaranteed opening-price lock. This is basis risk, not the same as absolute spot movement.
  5. The notes flag the source verbal early-hedge sign error: \(-F_0+(F_t-S_t)=-F_0-B\), buyer signed flow. Positive expenditure is \(F_0+B\). Never reuse the mistaken label.
  6. Zero-basis effective-price accounting does not remove interim calls. Daily settlement requires funding and the source ignores its interest; a final hedge outcome is not proof of no cash demand.

A purchase hedge, derived step by step

Suppose a buyer needs \(Nq\) units of the same underlying asset at maturity \(T\) and opens \(N\) matching long futures contracts at \(F_0\). Ignore transaction costs and interest on interim margin flows, as in the source's cash-flow arithmetic. By maturity the cumulative futures gain is \(Nq(F_T-F_0)\). Buying the asset spot costs \(NqS_T\). Net positive expenditure is therefore

\[E_T=NqS_T-Nq(F_T-F_0) =Nq\big[F_0+(S_T-F_T)\big] =Nq(F_0+B_T).\]

If convergence gives \(B_T=0\), net expenditure equals \(NqF_0\). A rise in the spot purchase price is offset by a futures gain; a fall reduces the spot cost but creates a futures loss. The hedge fixes the effective opening price under these matching assumptions. L10(EssentialsOfFuturesTrading).pptx, slide 10 FI01(Futures Contracts).doc, Ch. I, LibreOffice-rendered p. 7

The lecture instead writes signed cash flow with outflows negative:

\[(F_T-F_0)-S_T=-F_0-B_T=-F_0.\]

This is the same accounting with the opposite sign for expenditure. Defining the sign first avoids interpreting the negative amount as a negative purchase price. L10(EssentialsOfFuturesTrading).pptx, slide 10 FI01(Futures Contracts).doc, Ch. I, LibreOffice-rendered p. 7

The handout's five-day example ends at a spot and futures price of 900, after opening at 1,000. The long has lost 100 on futures, so buying spot for 900 costs 1,000 after that loss. This illustrates the hedge even though the futures leg itself is unprofitable. FI01(Futures Contracts).doc, Ch. I, LibreOffice-rendered p. 9

A sale hedge and early termination

A seller expecting to have the matching asset opens a short. Its futures gain is \(Nq(F_0-F_t)\), while its spot sale produces \(NqS_t\). The net receipt at time t is

\[R_t=NqS_t+Nq(F_0-F_t)=Nq(F_0+B_t).\]

The long buyer's positive expenditure at the same time is also \(Nq(F_0+B_t)\), while its signed cash flow is \(-Nq(F_0+B_t)\). At maturity the source's zero-basis condition fixes both purchase expenditure and sale revenue at the opening futures price. Before maturity, \(B_t\) is unknown when the hedge is opened. This is basis risk: the uncertainty about the future spot–futures difference remains after the broad price movement has been hedged. L10(EssentialsOfFuturesTrading).pptx, slide 10 FI01(Futures Contracts).doc, Ch. I, LibreOffice-rendered p. 7

The handout says hedging is “possible only” on delivery dates, but its following formula explicitly describes an early hedge with remaining basis. Its operational distinction is between the exact opening-price lock at zero basis and the imperfect early hedge exposed to basis risk. The early net-cost formula exists; it does not guarantee a predetermined cost. FI01(Futures Contracts).doc, Ch. I, LibreOffice-rendered p. 7 L10(EssentialsOfFuturesTrading).pptx, slide 10

The handout's final early-hedge line writes \(-F_0+(F_t-S_t)\) but labels it “futures price − basis”. With its own definition \(B_t=S_t-F_t\), the displayed expression is \(-F_0-B_t\): the long buyer's signed cash flow. Its positive expenditure is \(F_0+B_t\). The verbal label therefore has an apparent sign inconsistency; the distinction between signed cash flow and positive expenditure above preserves the algebra. FI01(Futures Contracts).doc, Ch. I, LibreOffice-rendered p. 6–7, final early-hedge line on p. 7

Origin: reused complete checked passage What to carry forward; fingerprint b3e4487925e9bd1ca9c2c341990863be4d2ff9f9a1e8e2211e1742e1bc4e5956. Heading depth alone is adapted.

What to carry forward

A futures trading gain is only one leg of a hedge. Combining it with the matching spot transaction gives the opening price plus the remaining basis. At zero basis the hedge fixes the price; at an earlier date it leaves basis risk. The result depends on matching quantity, asset and maturity and on the source's cash-flow assumptions. Margins and marking to market explains why funding obligations may still arise before the hedge reaches its final result. L10(EssentialsOfFuturesTrading).pptx, slide 5–6, 10

Misconceptions and correction

Signs: a losing futures leg treated as failed hedge; wrong hedge direction; buyer cost subtracted with wrong sign; early basis assumed zero. Correction: write two separate economic legs before combining; distinguish \(E\) from \(\mathrm{CF}\). Hint1: buyer needs gains when purchase price rises. Hint2: \(E=\text{spot cost}-\text{long gain}\). O007-C1 / v1: \(X=10\),\(F_0=10\),\(F_t=9\),\(S_t=8\). Give buyer \(E\)/\(\mathrm{CF}\) and interpret. Key long \(-10\),spot \(80\),\(E=90\),\(\mathrm{CF}=-90\),\(B=-1\),effective \(9\); loss on leg is offset by cheaper spot, early remaining basis. Pass sign and mechanism; partial missing explanation; fail \(E=70\) or futures loss proves hedge failure.

The sign alone is a tentative diagnosis; use the learner's reasoning to establish the confusion. Score the initial response before any hint. Hint 1 points to the named distinction; hint 2 points to the first correct step. After two ineffective hints show the prepared key, then pause if the fresh C1 response still fails. C1 checks the repaired component only and cannot replace a full independent check.

03

Key definitions and concepts

  • Excess burden: Distortionary welfare cost beyond a pure revenue transfer.
  • Revenue recycling: Using environmental-tax revenue, such as reducing another distortionary tax.
  • Strong double dividend: A nonenvironmental efficiency gain in addition to the environmental benefit, under the specified comparison.
04

Three goals of an environmental tax

The lecture separates getting prices right, changing behaviour and raising funds for public goods. Corrective pricing in the simple model uses the marginal external cost at the optimum, rather than total historical damage.

Behavioural incentives can induce resource saving and lower emissions. Revenue is an additional consequence whose use matters: the same gross collection can finance spending, be returned as a lump sum or reduce another tax.

Revenue is a transfer to government, not automatically an extra social benefit equal to the cash amount. The last distinction is explanatory synthesis consistent with the source's separate recycling and interaction effects.

For a given revenue requirement, optimal-tax theory seeks to minimize excess burden: the welfare loss caused by tax-induced distortions beyond the revenue transferred. The source contrasts a first-best lump-sum tax with second-best commodity taxation, where uniform rates are rarely optimal in the general setting.

A lump-sum tax is independent of the taxed agent's behaviour and induces no substitution effect, but may still have an income effect.

The lecture's Ramsey inverse-elasticity rule associates larger rates with less elastic demand: when quantity responds less strongly, a rate can raise revenue with less distortion on that margin. This is the source's simplified rule, not a general prescription ignoring cross-price responses, distribution or other constraints. The lecture does not derive its full demand-system assumptions.

05

Sandmo decomposition and the cost of public funds

In the lecture's Sandmo (1975) commodity-tax setting, clean goods follow a Ramsey component and the dirty-good tax combines Ramsey and Pigouvian components. The source explicitly excludes labour taxation from this displayed commodity-tax model. It writes

\[\theta_D=(1-\eta)R+\eta P.\]

Here \(\theta_D\) is its optimal dirty-commodity tax expression, \(R\) denotes the Ramsey component and \(P\) the Pigouvian component. The weight \(\eta=\lambda/\mu\) compares the marginal utility of income (\(\lambda\)) with the effect of a larger revenue requirement on social utility (\(\mu\)), as defined on the slide. \(R\) is unfortunately also used elsewhere for revenue requirement;

the context is needed to distinguish the meanings. This expression says the tax combines revenue and correction considerations, with a model-dependent weight.

It is not enough to compute a numerical rate without their values and a fully specified tax base.

The next slide introduces the marginal cost of public funds, \(MCPF=1/\eta\), and writes \(MCPF=-\Delta W/\Delta R\) for social welfare lost in raising an additional revenue amount. In that ratio \(\Delta R\) means additional public revenue, not the Ramsey component. Substituting \(\eta=1/MCPF\) gives

\[\theta_D=\left(1-\frac1{MCPF}\right)R+\frac1{MCPF}P.\]

The displayed slide then expands its components as

\[\theta_D=\left(1-\frac1{MCPF}\right)\left(-\frac1{\varepsilon_{DD}}\right) +\frac1{MCPF}\left(-N\frac{u_{ND}}{u_D}\right).\]

Lecture PDF p. 51. The formula has been checked visually: the Pigou term is \(-N u_{ND}/u_D\), with \(ND\) as a subscript on \(u\) and \(D\) as a subscript in the denominator.

The connection to the preceding inverse-elasticity slide identifies the first bracket as the Ramsey part. However, the lecture does not define \(N\), \(u_{ND}\) or \(u_D\), state the utility function from which they come, or specify whether \(\theta_D\) is a normalized/ad valorem or per-unit measure.

It also does not provide a derivation of the full expansion. These symbols are retained rather than assigned guessed meanings.

Only the weighted decomposition and its conditional algebraic case are self-contained from the supplied material.

If \(MCPF=1\), the Ramsey weight becomes zero and the Pigouvian weight one, so \(\theta_D=P\). The source describes this as indifference between public and private consumption. For any other value the weighting differs; the source does not supply empirical MCPF values or prove their sign/range. The decomposition should therefore not be treated as a universally calibrated environmental-tax rule.

06

Revenue recycling and the double dividend

The first or green dividend is an environmental improvement. A proposed second or blue dividend is higher employment or economic efficiency.

Three effects must be separated. The primary welfare gain is the environmental improvement correcting the pollution market.

The revenue-recycling effect can reduce distortions if proceeds cut existing distortionary taxes. The tax-interaction effect can increase distortions elsewhere: higher pollution-inclusive prices reduce real wages and can lower labour supply, depending on how leisure and dirty goods substitute or complement each other.

Reduced labour-tax receipts and falling environmental-tax receipts as avoidance improves can also require other revenue sources. The labour-supply discussion here extends beyond the commodity-only model used for the Sandmo display;

its effects cannot simply be inferred from that formula alone.

Weak double dividend compares two uses of a given environmental-tax revenue: reducing distortionary taxes versus returning a lump sum. The lecture says recycling through distortionary-tax cuts generally improves efficiency relative to the lump-sum option.

This comparison does not itself say the entire reform raises nonenvironmental welfare relative to no environmental tax. Strong double dividend makes that larger claim: overall economic efficiency rises in addition to the environmental gain. The lecture says theory is inconclusive and lists initial tax structure, substitutability, factor mobility and rigid wages as determinants.

A revenue-recycling gain can coexist with a larger tax-interaction loss.

6.1 The final slide's qualifications

Lecture p. 54 is image-based and adds qualifications absent from native text extraction.

The slide says the weak form has theoretical and simulation support but conditions its claim on there being only one distortionary tax. It then says it holds without restrictions under a first-best setting only, relates tax convergence to Ramsey's inverse-elasticity argument and states that weak separability requires equal commodity-tax rates.

These terse claims are not accompanied by a model, a definition of the separability relation, or a derivation. They coexist with p. 49's general statement that uniform commodity taxes are rarely optimal.

Read the equal-rate claim as a differently conditioned source statement; the supplied slides do not permit a complete theorem reconciling all of these assertions or a universal weak-double-dividend guarantee.

07

What can be applied and what needs further source material

The supplied material supports distinguishing marginal correction from revenue raising, explaining the weighted Ramsey/Pigou expression, evaluating its \(MCPF=1\) algebraic case and separating weak/strong double dividend from tax interaction. Numerical application of the expanded Sandmo formula and a proof of the p. 54 conditions require defined tax normalization, missing utility notation and full model assumptions.

Those strands remain source gaps, while the mechanism and conditional comparisons above are usable.

10.1 Question 1

Environmental gain 12, recycling gain 4, tax-interaction loss 6. What is nonenvironmental net change?

Reveal explanation

Nonenvironmental accounting is \(4-6=-2\).

10.2 Question 2

Same-revenue lump-sum return gives nonenvironmental change−6. How much does recycling improve that comparison?

Reveal explanation

Compare like revenue reforms:\(-2-(-6)=4\); the reform’s nonenvironmental level remains negative.

10.3 Question 3

At MCPF 1 in the supplied symbolic combination, what are Ramsey and Pigou weights?

Reveal explanation

Use \(1-1/1=0\) and \(1/1=1\), without inventing a calibrated tax base.

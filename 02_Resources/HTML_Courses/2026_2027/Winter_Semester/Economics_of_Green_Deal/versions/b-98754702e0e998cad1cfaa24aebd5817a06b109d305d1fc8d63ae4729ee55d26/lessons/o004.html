03

Key definitions and concepts

  • Externality question: Which economic activity affects others through a neglected environmental pathway.
  • Valuation question: How amenity and other welfare changes are assessed.
  • Assessment framework: A way to organize questions, distinct from a calibrated policy optimum.

3.1 Notation and distinctions

Terms and symbols are defined in the explanation below and exact cited sections. In network lessons \(P\) denotes production, \(C\) denotes consumption, \(U\) denotes utility, \(R\) denotes natural resources, \(\mathrm{ER}\) denotes exhaustible resources, \(\mathrm{RR}\) denotes renewable resources, \(h\) denotes harvest rate, \(y\) denotes yield/growth, \(W\) denotes waste, \(A\) denotes assimilative capacity.

These are qualitative labels; units are supplied explicitly only in our hypothetical comparisons.

The economic questions placed on the network

The same network with the source's topic labels: resources, growth and sustainability, externality, regulation, and valuation. Reproduced from 00_Intro.pptx, slide 5. The placement connects each topic to the physical or utility relationships it concerns.

Slide 5 overlays the course's economic questions on the physical network. Resources appears beside the resource branches. Growth and sustainability appears above the production-and-consumption relationship. Externality appears along feedback paths. Regulation appears around the production/waste part of the network. Valuation appears beside the amenity connections to utility. The overlay locates these topics but does not derive a policy instrument, an optimal pollution level, a valuation formula or a growth model.

An explanatory synthesis is to use this network as a sequence of questions. What resources support economic activity?

Does resource use outrun replenishment? How much waste reaches the environment relative to its capacity?

Which pathways change wellbeing, and how should those changes be assessed? The source's regulation and valuation labels point toward these policy and assessment questions.

Formal externality theory and instrument comparisons belong to later material and are not supplied by these introductory diagrams.

05

What the introductory framework establishes

The economy depends on resources and wider environmental services, while production and consumption generate waste and feedback to the environment and utility. The crucial conditions in the source diagram compare harvest with growth and waste with assimilative capacity.

These conditions make resource renewal and environmental capacity part of the analysis. The diagram offers a conceptual foundation for later policy assessment, while leaving quantitative models, measurements and climate science unspecified.

The scope note identifies the separate climate-foundation lecture referenced on slide 1. The uploaded deck supplies the environment–economy introduction, rather than the physical climate mechanisms, mitigation/adaptation/geoengineering explanations, or Kyoto-to-Paris history advertised for that lecture.

Course Orientation and Week 1 Scope

What will Economics of Green Deal examine, and what does the first week's uploaded material actually explain? The course studies environmental problems and climate change through economics, including how optimal regulation is defined and how policies' economic, social and environmental impacts can be quantified.

It gives special attention to decarbonisation scenarios and pathways, technology and social innovation. This note records that orientation and the assessment information, while identifying which Week 1 topics are supported by the supplied files.

Course navigation: Economics of Green Deal — Week 1. The Environment–Economy Nexus note explains the substantive introductory diagrams.

6.2 The course's central question

The English annotation frames the European Green Deal as a policy to transform the EU into a carbon-neutral, resource-efficient and competitive economy. The transition involves technology, behaviour and lifestyle, and the course asks how to assess its impacts on the economy, environment and people.

Environmental economics supplies the foundation, complemented by energy, transport and behavioural economics, energy-system modelling and environmental sciences. These are the course's stated aims and framing, rather than separately verified claims about current policy outcomes.

The introductory environment–economy framework explains why that assessment has several dimensions. Resources and environmental services support economic activity, waste feeds back into the environment, and both consumption and environmental amenities affect utility.

Consequently, the course's stated environmental, economic and social impact questions share a common conceptual setting. This connection is a synthesis of the annotation and the introductory diagrams.

08

Worked example

instruction only. Hypothetical proposal: firms increase production using renewable biomass while visitors value a forest. Use the framework to organize an assessment without choosing a policy.

Worked solution: Step 1: resources label concerns biomass inputs; ask h versus y rather than assuming renewable use is maintained.

Step 2: growth/sustainability concerns P/C and their resource pressures. Step 3: identify waste and feedback under externality, locate regulation around P/W, ask W versus A on comparable bases.

Step 4: valuation concerns the amenity-to-U path; consumption also has a positive U path and excess waste a negative one.

Step 5: this organizes economic/environmental/social questions without a net-welfare amount or optimal instrument. Physical climate mechanisms and Kyoto/Paris history require the missing lecture;

names in orientation are not teaching coverage.

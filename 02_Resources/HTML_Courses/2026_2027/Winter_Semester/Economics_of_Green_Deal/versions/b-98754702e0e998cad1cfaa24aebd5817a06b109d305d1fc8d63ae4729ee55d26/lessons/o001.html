Introduction

In this lesson, classify the four environmental functions.

2.1 What you’ll learn

  • From a supplied example
  • Identify resources, sinks/assimilative capacity, demand services and biospheric life-supporting services; preserve the slide’s resource taxonomy;

2.2 Before you begin

  • No earlier lesson in this course is required. Review the definitions below before the worked example.
03

Key definitions and concepts

  • Resource service: An environmental input to production or consumption.
  • Demand service: Direct environmental enjoyment, such as forest recreation, distinct from supplying a material input.
  • Sink service: Receipt or assimilation of undesirable outputs, with capacity limits.
  • Biospheric service: A life-supporting natural process, such as the hydrologic cycle.

3.1 Notation and distinctions

Terms and symbols are defined in the explanation below and exact cited sections. In network lessons \(P\) denotes production, \(C\) denotes consumption, \(U\) denotes utility, \(R\) denotes natural resources, \(\mathrm{ER}\) denotes exhaustible resources, \(\mathrm{RR}\) denotes renewable resources, \(h\) denotes harvest rate, \(y\) denotes yield/growth, \(W\) denotes waste, \(A\) denotes assimilative capacity.

These are qualitative labels; units are supplied explicitly only in our hypothetical comparisons.

04

Four environmental functions

The slides distinguish four ways in which the environment matters to the economy. These functions can coexist in the same place. The forest example shows the distinction particularly clearly: wood is a resource, while aesthetic, cultural and recreational enjoyment belongs to the separate category of demand services. This comparison is an explanatory synthesis of the examples on slide 2.

4.1 Resources

Resources provide inputs or possibilities for economic activity. Slide 2 distinguishes non-degradable resources, with sunshine, wind and waves as examples, from degradable resources.

Within the latter group it distinguishes renewable resources, such as wood and biomass, from non-renewable or exhaustible resources, such as fossil fuels and minerals. It also lists space as a resource.

Keep this source taxonomy when interpreting the later diagram: the slide uses “renewable” for the wood/biomass category and separately lists sunshine, wind and waves as non-degradable. It does not supply a broader taxonomy or a formal definition of “degradable.”

The distinction becomes economically meaningful when resource use is compared with replenishment. An exhaustible resource can be reduced by extraction.

For renewable resources, the diagram separately represents harvesting above and below yield or growth, so “renewable” alone does not establish that a particular pattern of use maintains the resource. The relevant question is how fast the resource is used relative to how fast it grows.

4.2 Sinks and assimilative capacity

A sink is a place where undesirable outputs, including emissions and wastes, may be placed. The source describes this function through assimilative capacity: the environment's capacity to receive or assimilate those outputs. The diagram later compares waste with that capacity. This makes waste disposal part of the environment–economy relationship, alongside resource extraction.

The capacity comparison matters because the diagram assigns different consequences to waste below capacity and waste above capacity. The existence of a sink therefore does not establish that every amount of waste has the same consequences. The source supplies a qualitative threshold comparison; it does not quantify a safe emissions level, a pollutant-specific assimilation rate, or the time needed for assimilation.

4.3 Demand services

The environment also supplies services people value directly. Slide 2 names aesthetic, cultural and recreational services, such as those provided by a forest. In the full diagram, the environmental resource node has a positive amenity connection to utility. Thus the environment contributes to wellbeing through more than goods produced by firms and consumed by households.

Utility, denoted by \(U\), is the diagram's representation of wellbeing or benefit. The source identifies utility flows but gives no numerical utility scale, utility function, or valuation method. The positive amenity arrow establishes a direction of effect in this schematic framework; it does not report a measured monetary benefit.

4.4 Global biospheric life-supporting services

The fourth category concerns the systems that support life more generally. The source names the climate system, hydrologic cycle and soil production.

These services place the production-and-consumption relationship in a wider biospheric setting. They explain why environmental economics considers systemic conditions as well as individual resource inputs and enjoyable amenities.

This final sentence is a synthesis of the category's role in the introductory framework.

05

Connect the reasoning

  1. Ask whether the environmental feature supplies an input or possibility for economic activity: this is a resource. Wood/biomass are degradable renewable resources; fossil fuels/minerals are degradable non-renewable/exhaustible resources. Sunshine, wind and waves are separately classified as non-degradable in this source; space is also a resource.
  2. Ask whether the relationship concerns receiving undesirable outputs: this is the sink function, interpreted through assimilative capacity. A sink does not imply unlimited capacity.
  3. Ask whether people value aesthetic, cultural or recreational services directly: these are demand services. Forest recreation differs from timber production even at the same site.
  4. Ask whether the relationship concerns broad life-supporting systems: climate, the hydrologic cycle and soil production belong to the biospheric category.
  5. Keep categories distinct by mechanism while allowing coexistence. The source supplies this taxonomy, not a general formal definition of degradability; do not replace its wording with another taxonomy.
06

Worked example

instruction only. An hypothetical forest example combines timber, recreation, receipt of undesirable outputs, and the hydrologic cycle. Classify each and compare wood, fossil fuels and wind in the source taxonomy.

Worked solution: Step 1: timber supplies an input, so resource. Step 2: recreation gives direct enjoyment, so demand service.

Step 3: receipt/assimilation of outputs is sink service, without an unlimited-capacity claim. Step 4: the hydrologic cycle is a biospheric life-supporting service.

Wood is degradable renewable; fossil fuels are degradable exhaustible;

wind is non-degradable in the slide. The same place supports several relationships;

its identity does not force one exclusive category.

Check your reasoning: an offline quiz

Choose one answer per question. Check individual answers or check the whole quiz. Each option includes feedback on the reasoning, and everything works offline.

8.1 Question 1

Which pairing classifies forest timber and recreation?

Reveal explanation

Classify the relationship with the economy rather than the place alone.

8.2 Question 2

Receiving waste and supporting the hydrologic cycle illustrate what functions?

Reveal explanation

Waste receipt is a sink function; the hydrologic cycle supports life.

8.3 Question 3

Which source-taxonomy comparison is correct?

Reveal explanation

Use the declared taxonomy without making the categories interchangeable.

03

Key definitions and concepts

  • Bargaining range: Transfers acceptable to both sides under the declared rights and alternatives.
  • Efficiency: Maximizing the specified aggregate surplus.
  • Equity: The distribution of outcomes, not determined by equal total surplus.
04

Coasean bargaining and its limitations

The lecture illustrates bargaining with radio stations whose broadcasts interfere. With enforceable rights, transferable money, feasible ways of avoiding the harm and no transaction costs, the station with the higher-valued use can compensate the other to obtain that use.

In the usual Coasean argument, bargaining reaches an efficient outcome regardless of initial rights, under the stated ideal conditions and with the lecture's uniqueness qualification. Initial rights still govern who pays whom and how surplus is distributed.

Transaction costs include finding and coordinating participants, negotiating, monitoring and enforcing agreements. The lecture's factory facing a thousand landowners illustrates why the two-person ideal becomes difficult: individual victims can wait for others to finance the improvement.

Missing environmental-quality rights and undepletable public-bad effects add difficulties. With transaction costs, the attainable allocation can depend on initial rights.

The lecture's “Second Coase theorem” recommends institutions that reduce reallocation costs, while admitting that governments cannot know the highest-valued use without cost. This title is the lecture's wording, not a reason to omit its qualifications.

05

Efficiency and equity lead to different comparisons

An efficient tax sets the marginal incentive; it need not equal the total unpaid bill.

For an increasing MEC curve, the rectangle \(Q^*t^*\) can exceed the area of total external damage. In the chapter's linear diagram it is twice the optimal external damage \(A\).

With constant MEC, marginal and average damage coincide. Thus the polluter-pays principle is ambiguous until one specifies payment of total damage, marginal incentive, or both.

Willingness to pay or accept also depends on income: the same physical exposure can receive a larger money valuation for a richer receptor. Locating pollution where measured money damage is low can therefore conflict with equity concerns.

Table 13.1, , printed p. 208. The efficiency question needs marginal external cost, individual aggregation and both intra- and intersectoral harm. The unpaid-bill question needs total external cost plus defensive outlays, sectoral aggregation and costs shifted outside the sector. Footnotes retain the qualifications on insurance and taxes.

A fixed annual insurance premium or ownership tax has little direct effect on the next kilometre driven. It can nevertheless matter to unpaid-bill accounting, with the table's restrictions: insurance payments must be compared with costs imposed outside the sector;

ownership taxes cover unpaid costs only above infrastructure expenditure; fuel taxes used as Pigouvian corrections need a differential above ordinary indirect taxation, apart from second-best taxation questions.

Existing transfers should therefore be evaluated against the actual research question.

Figure 13.2, , printed p. 209. Its letters belong to this figure, separately from Figure 13.1 and the lecture diagram. Here \(MNPB=MPB-MPC\) is marginal net private benefit in money per unit of activity \(Q\), and \(MEC\) is marginal external cost in the same units.

The private choice \(Q^0\) has \(MNPB=0\); the social choice \(Q^*\) has \(MNPB=MEC=t^*\).

The labelled areas integrate these marginal quantities and therefore measure money, rather than money per unit.

To read the letters, start left of \(Q^*\). Area \(A\) lies between \(MNPB\) and the horizontal tax level \(t^*\); \(B\) lies between \(t^*\) and \(MEC\); \(C\) is below \(MEC\).

Thus net private benefit through \(Q^*\) is \(A+B+C\), damage is \(C\), the tax rectangle is \(B+C\), and social welfare is \(A+B\). Right of \(Q^*\), \(D\) is below \(MNPB\) between \(Q^*\) and \(Q^0\), while \(E\) is between \(MEC\) and \(MNPB\).

Continuing to \(Q^0\) adds private benefit \(D\) and damage \(D+E\), lowering social welfare by \(E\). Unrestricted private benefit is consequently \(A+B+C+D\), damage is \(C+D+E\), and their difference is \(A+B-E\).

This construction makes every transfer in Table 13.2 interpretable without borrowing another diagram's letters.

Table 13.2, , printed p. 210. It compares distribution under a single producer, a single victim unable to defend itself, and the Figure 13.2 area convention. Parentheses denote transfers between producer and victim; square brackets denote transfers to the regulator. In the simple model all optimizing schemes yield social welfare \(A+B\), while non-intervention yields \(A+B-E\).

A quantity restriction leaves producer welfare \(A+B+C\) and victim welfare \(-C\). A Pigouvian tax transfers \(B+C\) to the regulator, leaving producer welfare \(A\), victim welfare \(-C\) and the same social total.

Direct compensation transfers \(C\) to the victim, leaving it at zero and the producer at \(A+B\). A merger gathers the interests into the common total.

If the producer owns the right, the victim pays to reduce production: the two extreme-bargainer cases transfer \(D\) or \(D+E\) and leave the producer above its unrestricted welfare in one case. If the victim owns the right, the producer pays between \(C\) and \(A+B+C\) in the table's extreme cases.

The upper payment \(D+E\) assumes truthful bargaining: chapter note 12 excludes a producer threatening output above the private choice \(Q^0\) to extract a larger payment. These are alternative distributional bounds, not simultaneous payments.

The chapter consequently explains why producer and victim rank schemes differently and why political support for standards can differ from support for taxes. Revenue allocation is assumed ignored by both actors in the table's tax row.

The tax-versus-quantity equivalence here relies on a deliberately simple model. Chapter note 8 warns that uncertainty and heterogeneity can break it; tradable permits also introduce a market absent from a plain quantity restriction. The instrument note explains cost effectiveness and enforcement, while the taxation note adds pre-existing fiscal distortions.

Worked example

Prompt: Hypothetical Figure 13.2 area convention (distinct from earlier diagrams): \(A=B=C=D=10\) money and \(E=20\). A producer and one victim can bargain with enforceable rights.

Define each area geometrically through \(Q^{*}\)/\(Q_0\), \(\mathrm{MNPB}\) and \(\mathrm{MEC}\); derive unrestricted/private/damage/social totals.

Compute producer/victim/regulator welfare under restriction, Pigouvian tax and direct compensation at \(Q^{*}\). Derive min/max transfers and resulting welfare when producer owns the right and when victim owns it.

Explain Coase conditions, producer-truthfulness upper-bound qualification, why equal efficiency need not mean equal equity, and marginal-tax versus total-unpaid-bill/valuation limits.

Steps and interpretation: Left of \(Q^{*}\): \(A\) between \(\mathrm{MNPB}\) and horizontal \(t^{*}\), \(B\) between \(t^{*}\) and \(\mathrm{MEC}\), \(C\) below \(\mathrm{MEC}\). From \(Q^{*}\) to \(Q_0\): \(D\) below \(\mathrm{MNPB}\), \(E\) between \(\mathrm{MEC}\) and \(\mathrm{MNPB}\).

Private welfare at \(Q^{*}\) is \(A+B+C=30\); damage \(C=10\);

social \(A+B=20\); tax rectangle \(B+C=20\).

Unrestricted private welfare is \(A+B+C+D = 40\), damage \(C+D+E=40\), social \(A+B-E=0\). Restriction: producer \(30\), victim \(-10\);

tax: producer \(A=10\), victim \(-10\), regulator \(B+C=20\); compensation transfer \(C=10\): producer \(20\), victim \(0\), same total \(20\).

Producer owns the right: victim pays \(D=10\) minimum (producer’s lost private gain) through \(D+E=30\) maximum (victim’s avoided damage); at \(Q^{*}\) producer \(A+B+C+\text{transfer}\) ranges from \(40\) to \(60\), victim \(-C-\text{transfer}\) ranges from \(-20\) to \(-40\);

total \(A+B=20\) always. Upper bound requires truthful producer not threatening above \(Q_0\).

Victim owns the right: producer pays from \(C=10\) to \(A+B+C=30\), so producer welfare ranges from \(20\) to \(0\) and victim welfare ranges from \(0\) to \(20\). These are alternative bargaining extremes, not simultaneous payments.

Coase needs enforceable rights, feasible money transfers and sufficiently low transaction/coordination costs; many victims/public-bad free riding can obstruct negotiation.

Rights change transfers, not the ideal efficient \(Q^{*}\), but transaction costs can change attainable allocation. Efficiency does not determine fair distribution;

marginal incentive and total damage differ (tax rectangle \(20\) versus total damage \(10\) here), while income-sensitive willingness-to-pay can conflict with equity. A fixed insurance/ownership payment may compensate but not price another kilometre;

sector unpaid-bill accounting needs its own boundary and defence expenditures.

Check your reasoning: an offline quiz

Choose one answer per question. Check individual answers or check the whole quiz. Each option includes feedback on the reasoning, and everything works offline.

8.1 Question 1

Producer owns the right. Lost private gain from restriction 10; avoided damage 30. What is the specified transfer range?

Reveal explanation

Minimum compensates producer loss; maximum is victim’s avoided damage, under the truthful-threat qualification.

8.2 Question 2

At Q*, restriction, tax and compensation all yield total 20 in the example. Are distributions identical?

Reveal explanation

Track every actor’s welfare and cancel pure transfers in the total.

8.3 Question 3

What can obstruct the ideal rights-independent efficient bargaining outcome?

Reveal explanation

The ideal result requires enforceable rights, feasible transfers and sufficiently low bargaining costs.

Random walk: constant mean does not imply stationarity

Set \(\rho=1\). Repeated substitution gives

\[x_t=x_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t=x_0+\sum_{j=1}^{t}\varepsilon_j.\]

For \(x_0\) independent of the innovations,

\[E[x_t]=E[x_0],\qquad \operatorname{Var}(x_t)=\operatorname{Var}(x_0)+t\sigma_\varepsilon^2.\]

If \(x_0=c\) is fixed, these become \(E[x_t]=c\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(x_t)=t\sigma_\varepsilon^2\). Increasing variance prevents covariance stationarity even with a constant mean. Source qualification: p.26 first allows an independent initial value but then writes \(E[x_0]=x_0\) and drops \(\operatorname{Var}(x_0)\). Those reductions require fixed \(x_0\); for random \(x_0\) retain its expectation and variance. The textbook distinguishes the cases explicitly. 2026_Lecture3_print.pdf, physical PDF p. 26 Wooldridge, Introductory Econometrics, 5th ed., printed p. 392 (PDF p. 420)

Conditional prediction is

\[E[x_{t+h}\mid x_t]=x_t.\]

A shock to today's level enters every future level with coefficient one. This contrasts with \(\rho^h\) for stable AR(1), and explains permanent versus fading shocks. A random walk is one example of a unit-root process. A high sample lag-one correlation is suggestive of persistence, but it does not itself prove an exact unit root. 2026_Lecture3_print.pdf, physical PDF p. 27, 30

Drift and deterministic trend are different

For a random walk with drift \(a\),

\[x_t=a+x_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t=x_0+at+\sum_{j=1}^{t}\varepsilon_j,\]

\[E[x_t]=E[x_0]+at,\quad \operatorname{Var}(x_t)=\operatorname{Var}(x_0)+t\sigma_\varepsilon^2,\quad E[x_{t+h}\mid x_t]=x_t+ah.\]

The deterministic mean trend \(at\) does not remove accumulated random shocks. Subtracting \(at\) leaves a random walk. By contrast, a trend-stationary process \(z_t=b_0+b_1t+v_t\) has stationary weakly dependent deviations \(v_t\); removing the correct deterministic trend exposes those deviations. The lecture's “include a trend” reassurance on p.18 concerns that case and still requires valid regression assumptions. It is not a remedy for any series that rises over time. Persistence can exist without a visible trend (the lecture names interest rates, inflation and unemployment), and a trend can exist without persistent stochastic shocks. 2026_Lecture3_print.pdf, physical PDF p. 18, 28, 30 Wooldridge, Introductory Econometrics, 5th ed., printed p. 394 (PDF p. 422) Wooldridge, Introductory Econometrics, 5th ed., printed p. 395 (PDF p. 423)

Integration and first differences

In this course's framing, an \(I(0)\) process has the weak-dependence properties needed for ordinary limit-theorem reasoning; an \(I(1)\) level requires one difference to obtain such a process. For the random walk,

\[\Delta x_t=x_t-x_{t-1}=\varepsilon_t,\]

so differences are i.i.d. and \(I(0)\) even though levels are \(I(1)\). With drift the difference is \(a+\varepsilon_t\). General \(I(1)\) differences need not be i.i.d.; they may retain weak serial dependence. Differencing changes the modeled variable from its level to its period-to-period change, so coefficients must be interpreted accordingly. It does not automatically cure endogeneity or every inference problem. 2026_Lecture3_print.pdf, physical PDF p. 29 Wooldridge, Introductory Econometrics, 5th ed., printed p. 396 (PDF p. 424)

For a deterministic trend, \(\Delta z_t=b_1+\Delta v_t\), which also removes its changing mean but may alter short-run dependence. Choosing between detrending and differencing therefore requires a process argument; “both remove a visible trend” is insufficient. Online verification: Hyndman and Athanasopoulos, Forecasting: Principles and Practice, 3rd ed., §9.1 “Stationarity and differencing,” subsections “Differencing” and “Random walk model,” confirms the random-walk transformation and warns that unnecessary differencing can introduce autocorrelation. Consulted author textbook section. 2026_Lecture3_print.pdf, physical PDF p. 18, 29–30

What the lecture does and does not test

Physical p.30 advises looking at lag-one correlation and names Dickey–Fuller and augmented Dickey–Fuller tests, referring to textbook Chapter18.2. The supplied slides do not provide those tests' regressions, null distributions, critical values or worked applications, so naming them is not a prepared test procedure. The instruction “Detrend first!” is supported here only as a warning that a deterministic trend can inflate estimated autocorrelation; it does not mean detrending makes every unit-root series stationary. Wooldridge, 5th ed., printed p.398 (PDF p.426) Formal unit-root-testing instruction beyond the source's pointers remains outside this note. 2026_Lecture3_print.pdf, physical PDF p. 30 Wooldridge, Introductory Econometrics, 5th ed., printed p. 397 (PDF p. 425)

Spurious regression is the danger that unrelated persistent series in levels display a misleading apparent relationship, potentially accompanied by large fit or seemingly significant usual test statistics. Shared-looking movements in one observed history need not represent an economic connection. If an accumulated random shock is mistaken for a deterministic trend, removing a fitted line still leaves the stochastic persistence; the weak-dependence conditions justifying the ordinary inference above have not thereby been restored. This is the warning on lecture p.30, rather than a claim that every regression containing an \(I(1)\) variable is necessarily spurious. The lecture postpones formal analysis. 2026_Lecture3_print.pdf, physical PDF p.30 Wooldridge, 5th ed., printed p.395 (PDF p.423)

Worked example

O017-W1, item_version 1, worked_example. Prompt: A random walk with drift satisfies \(x_t=a+x_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t\), with \(a=0.5\), i.i.d. zero-mean innovations of variance 4 independent of \(x_0\), \(E[x_0]=2\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(x_0)=3\). Derive the accumulated representation, mean/variance at \(t=5\), and the 2-period forecast from current level 10. Derive first differences and classify levels/differences in the course’s \(I(1)/I(0)\) framing. Compare removing a deterministic trend from \(z_t=b_0+b_1t+v_t\) with removing drift from this random walk. Explain why sample lag-one correlation, a fitted trend, or high regression fit cannot establish stationarity, a unit root, causality or valid usual inference. State the scope of the lecture’s DF/ADF pointers.

Complete solution and essential rubric: Iteration yields \(x_t=x_0+at+\sum_{j=1}^t\varepsilon_j\), so \(E[x_t]=E[x_0]+at=4.50000000\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(x_t)=\operatorname{Var}(x_0)+t\sigma_\varepsilon^2=23.00000000\). Independent random initialization requires retaining its variance/expectation; dropping the variance is valid only if fixed. The forecast is \(E[x_{t+h}\mid x_t]=x_t+ah=11.00000000\); a shock’s level coefficient remains one. \(\Delta x_t=a+\varepsilon_t\) has mean 0.5, variance 4, and is i.i.d. \(I(0)\) here; levels are \(I(1)\) and nonstationary because variance grows. General \(I(1)\) differences can retain weak dependence and need not be i.i.d. Subtracting \(at\) leaves accumulated shocks, whereas removing the correct deterministic trend from the trend-stationary \(z_t\) exposes stationary weakly dependent \(v_t\). Differencing changes interpretation from level to change and does not automatically fix endogeneity/inference. Large lag-one correlation is a persistence diagnostic, not proof of an exact unit root; detrending is not a universal unit-root remedy. Unrelated persistent levels can produce misleading high fit/usual significance, without proving every regression involving an \(I(1)\) variable is spurious.

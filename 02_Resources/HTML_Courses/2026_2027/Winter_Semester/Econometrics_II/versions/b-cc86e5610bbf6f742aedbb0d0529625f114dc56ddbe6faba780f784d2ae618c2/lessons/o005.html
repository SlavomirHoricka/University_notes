03

Motivation, intuition, and definitions

Derive the simple-slope conditional variance, state the precise BLUE comparison class, calculate a conventional slope standard error/\(t\) statistic and degrees of freedom, and discriminate which assumptions support each property. The question is what the available information lets us calculate or conclude, and which extra conditions the conclusion needs.

BLUE means best linear unbiased estimator conditional on \(X\). Linear means linear in \(y\) observations with \(X\)-determined weights; unbiased is the comparison restriction; best is minimum conditional variance within that class, coefficient by coefficient. It is not minimum variance among all estimators/all loss functions. Homoskedasticity is common finite conditional disturbance variance \(\sigma^2\). Normality is additional for the stated exact finite-sample null \(t\) and \(F\) distributions; \(F\) addresses joint coefficient restrictions, whose calculation is outside this bounded lesson.

04

Explanation steps

  1. In the slope decomposition the error is a weighted sum of \(u\) with weights \((x_i-\bar x)/S_{xx}\). Variance of this sum has squared weight variance terms and cross-observation covariance terms.
  2. Sampling conditions remove the cross terms; common conditional variance \(\sigma^2\) gives \(Var(\hat\beta_1\mid X)=\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2\sigma^2/S_{xx}^2=\sigma^2/S_{xx}\). More \(x\) variation improves precision, more noise worsens it.
  3. With MLR1–5, the Gauss–Markov theorem gives BLUE in the stated linear unbiased class; this explanation calculates OLS variance, not a full proof of the theorem. No normality needed for BLUE.
  4. Estimate unknown \(\sigma^2\) by \(SSE/(n-k -1)\), giving simple-slope estimated variance \(\hat\sigma^2/S_{xx}\) and \(\mathrm{se}\) its square root. \(k\) counts nonconstant regressors: residual \(\mathrm{df}=n-k -1\), not \(n-k\).
  5. For null \(\beta_{1,0}\) use \(t=(\text{estimated slope}-\text{null})/\mathrm{se}\). Under the full six classical assumptions, compare to the \(t\) distribution with those \(\mathrm{df}\) for exact finite-sample inference. Rejecting a zero slope does not establish exogeneity/causality. An \(F\) test addresses joint restrictions; no joint-test arithmetic is claimed without further prepared notes.
  6. With heteroskedasticity but first four assumptions maintained, unbiasedness survives and consistency may survive with regularity; stated BLUE/conventional variance and exact classical \(t\) claims no longer follow. With MLR1–5 but nonnormality, BLUE still holds while the stated exact classical normal/\(t\)/\(F\) distributions are not established. This lesson supplies no unsupported replacement variance/test method.

Efficiency: specify the comparison class

The Gauss–Markov theorem says that under linearity in parameters, random sampling, no perfect collinearity, zero conditional mean, and homoskedasticity, OLS is BLUE conditional on \(X\): the best linear unbiased estimator. “Linear” means linear in the dependent-variable observations, with weights determined by \(X\). “Unbiased” restricts the comparison to estimators centered on the target parameter. “Best” means smallest conditional variance within that class, coefficient by coefficient. This does not claim that every conceivable estimator has a larger variance, or that OLS minimizes all possible loss functions. Lecture 1, PDF pp. 23–24

For the simple-regression slope, the decomposition provides an explanatory variance calculation. Under conditional independence and common disturbance variance \(\sigma^2\),

\[\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta_1\mid X) =\frac{1}{S_{xx}^2}\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2\sigma^2 =\frac{\sigma^2}{S_{xx}}.\]

Cross-observation covariance terms vanish under the sampling assumptions. More variation in \(x\) supplies a more precise slope, while more disturbance variation reduces precision. This equation answers the seminar's slope-variance question and explains why homoskedasticity matters for the usual standard error. With heteroskedasticity, zero conditional mean can still preserve unbiasedness and suitable conditions can preserve consistency, but the stated BLUE theorem and conventional variance formula no longer follow. Lecture 1, PDF p. 24; Seminar 1, PDF p. 2, slope-variance task

Normality and hypothesis tests add another requirement

The lecture adds normality to the preceding assumptions to obtain a conditional normal distribution for OLS coefficients and exact null \(t\) and \(F\) distributions. This is a finite-sample inference statement, separate from unbiasedness, consistency, and BLUE. For a null slope value \(\beta_{1,0}\),

\[t=\frac{\hat\beta_1-\beta_{1,0}}{\operatorname{se}(\hat\beta_1)}.\]

Under the classical assumptions, in a regression with \(k\) nonconstant regressors this statistic has a \(t\) distribution with \(n-k-1\) residual degrees of freedom under the null. An \(F\) statistic addresses joint coefficient restrictions. The formula and degrees of freedom are an agent expansion of the lecture's null-distribution statement, used in the seminar's requested slope test. A small standard error or a large absolute \(t\) statistic cannot establish exogeneity; inference is conditional on the maintained model assumptions. Lecture 1, PDF pp. 25–26; Seminar 1, PDF p. 2

Distinctions to retain

Question Property Week 1 OLS conditions and qualification
Is the repeated-sample center correct? Unbiasedness First four assumptions; an individual estimate can still be far from the parameter.
Does error become improbable as \(n\) grows? Consistency First four assumptions are sufficient with regularity; weaker orthogonality moments can replace zero conditional mean.
Is the estimator most precise in the stated class? BLUE efficiency First five assumptions; the class is linear unbiased estimators conditional on \(X\).
Are the stated finite-sample null distributions exact? Classical inference First six assumptions; normality is an additional requirement.

These distinctions summarize Lecture 1, PDF pp. 16–26. The transport-demand seminar (ten-city calculation; CSV analysis and report; diagnostics) turns them into interpretation questions: fit the model, identify what the evidence measures, and assess which assumptions can plausibly support it.

Worked example

O005-W v1, hypothetical \(n=10, k=1, S_{xx}=8, SSE=128\), \(\text{slope}=-4\), null \(0\): \(\mathrm{df}=8\); \(\hat\sigma^2=16\); slope variance \(2\); \(\mathrm{se}=\sqrt{2}\approx1.414214\); \(t\approx -2.828427\). Under MLR1–6, \(\lvert t\rvert>2.306\) (the note's approximate two-sided 5% \(t_8\) cutoff), so reject \(0\). Under only MLR1–5, BLUE is justified but that exact reference cannot simply be assumed. Doubling \(S_{xx}\) at the same \(\sigma^2\) halves variance, reducing \(\mathrm{se}\) by \(\sqrt{2}\) rather than half.

Misconceptions and correction

Sign: BLUE means best among every estimator; variance and \(\mathrm{se}\) confused; \(t\) ignores nonzero null; or homoskedasticity confused with normality. Correction: theorem restricts the class; standard error is square root of variance; subtract the null; homoskedasticity concerns spread, normality shape. Hint1: name class and write \(t\) numerator. Hint2: calculate residual \(\mathrm{df}\) and square root explicitly.

Stable AR(1): assumptions before moments

An autoregression of order one obeys

\[x_t=\rho x_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t,\]

with i.i.d. zero-mean innovations of finite variance \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2>0\). Future innovations must be independent of the initial value/past. The stability condition is \(|\rho|<1\). To derive the lecture's constant moments from the start, take a covariance-stationary initialization; the recurrence and stability alone do not guarantee that an arbitrary fixed or random \(x_0\) has the stationary distribution. The lecture omits that initialization qualification on p.15; the textbook explicitly assumes covariance stationarity in the displayed moment argument. 2026_Lecture3_print.pdf, physical PDF p. 15–16 Wooldridge, Introductory Econometrics, 5th ed., printed p. 383 (PDF p. 411)

Under stationary initialization, the mean satisfies \(\mu=\rho\mu\), giving \(\mu=0\) because \(\rho\ne1\). Independence of the innovation from the past gives

\[\sigma_x^2=\rho^2\sigma_x^2+\sigma_\varepsilon^2,\qquad \sigma_x^2=\frac{\sigma_\varepsilon^2}{1-\rho^2}.\]

The positive denominator is why a nondegenerate finite stationary variance is compatible with \(|\rho|<1\). A recurrence started instead at fixed zero has

\[\operatorname{Var}(x_t)=\sigma_\varepsilon^2\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}\rho^{2j}=\sigma_\varepsilon^2\frac{1-\rho^{2t}}{1-\rho^2},\]

which approaches the stationary variance but is not equal to it at every finite date. This last expression is agent-derived qualification: repeatedly substitute the recurrence, then add variances of independent shocks. It shows exactly what the missing initialization changes. 2026_Lecture3_print.pdf, physical PDF p. 15–16 Wooldridge, Introductory Econometrics, 5th ed., printed p. 383 (PDF p. 411)

Why AR(1) correlation fades

Substitution over \(h\) future periods gives

\[x_{t+h}=\rho^h x_t+\sum_{j=1}^{h}\rho^{h-j}\varepsilon_{t+j}.\]

Future innovations have zero covariance with \(x_t\), so under the stationary moments

\[\operatorname{Cov}(x_t,x_{t+h})=\rho^h\sigma_x^2,\qquad \operatorname{Corr}(x_t,x_{t+h})=\rho^h.\]

Thus correlation tends to zero geometrically when \(|\rho|<1\). Negative \(\rho\) alternates the sign; a \(\rho\) near one decays slowly in a short sample but remains stable. Under zero conditional-mean future innovations, \(E[x_{t+h}\mid x_t]=\rho^h x_t\) approaches zero. This is the mechanism behind fading shocks, rather than a claim that adjacent values are independent. 2026_Lecture3_print.pdf, physical PDF p. 17, 27 Wooldridge, Introductory Econometrics, 5th ed., printed p. 384 (PDF p. 412)

Worked example

O016-W1, item_version 1, worked_example. Prompt: For \(x_t=\rho x_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t\), take \(\rho=0.5\), i.i.d. zero-mean innovations of variance 3, independent of the initial value/past. Under covariance-stationary initialization derive mean, variance, lag-2 covariance/correlation and forecast from current \(x_t=8\). Compare with initialization \(x_0=0\) by computing variance at date 2. Explain stability, shock decay and the distinction between a near-unit-root stable coefficient and an exact random walk. Does the recurrence with \(|\rho|<1\) alone force stationary finite-date moments from an arbitrary initialization?

Complete solution and essential rubric: With \(|\rho|<1\) and stationary initialization, \(\mu=\rho\mu\) implies \(\mu=0\). Independence gives \(\sigma_x^2=\rho^2\sigma_x^2+\sigma_\varepsilon^2\), hence \(\sigma_x^2=4.00000000\). Iteration gives \(x_{t+h}=\rho^h x_t+\sum_{j=1}^h\rho^{h-j}\varepsilon_{t+j}\). Future innovations have zero covariance/conditional mean given the past, so lag \(2\) covariance is \(\rho^2\sigma_x^2=1.00000000\), correlation \(\rho^2=0.25000000\) and conditional forecast \(\rho^2(8)=2.00000000\). With fixed zero initialization, variance at date \(2\) is \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2(1-\rho^{2t})/(1-\rho^2)=3.75000000\); it approaches but generally is not the stationary variance at a finite date. Thus stability/recurrence alone does not supply stationary initialization. Powers decay to zero; negative \(\rho\) alternates signs and a value near one decays slowly without equaling a unit root. At \(\rho\) one shocks accumulate instead. Constant population moments and sample appearances are distinct. Random walk: constant mean does not imply stationarity

Set \(\rho=1\). Repeated substitution gives

\[x_t=x_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t=x_0+\sum_{j=1}^{t}\varepsilon_j.\]

For \(x_0\) independent of the innovations,

\[E[x_t]=E[x_0],\qquad \operatorname{Var}(x_t)=\operatorname{Var}(x_0)+t\sigma_\varepsilon^2.\]

If \(x_0=c\) is fixed, these become \(E[x_t]=c\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(x_t)=t\sigma_\varepsilon^2\). Increasing variance prevents covariance stationarity even with a constant mean. Source qualification: p.26 first allows an independent initial value but then writes \(E[x_0]=x_0\) and drops \(\operatorname{Var}(x_0)\). Those reductions require fixed \(x_0\); for random \(x_0\) retain its expectation and variance. The textbook distinguishes the cases explicitly. 2026_Lecture3_print.pdf, physical PDF p. 26 Wooldridge, Introductory Econometrics, 5th ed., printed p. 392 (PDF p. 420)

Conditional prediction is

\[E[x_{t+h}\mid x_t]=x_t.\]

A shock to today's level enters every future level with coefficient one. This contrasts with \(\rho^h\) for stable AR(1), and explains permanent versus fading shocks. A random walk is one example of a unit-root process. A high sample lag-one correlation is suggestive of persistence, but it does not itself prove an exact unit root. 2026_Lecture3_print.pdf, physical PDF p. 27, 30

Drift and deterministic trend are different

For a random walk with drift \(a\),

\[x_t=a+x_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t=x_0+at+\sum_{j=1}^{t}\varepsilon_j,\]

\[E[x_t]=E[x_0]+at,\quad \operatorname{Var}(x_t)=\operatorname{Var}(x_0)+t\sigma_\varepsilon^2,\quad E[x_{t+h}\mid x_t]=x_t+ah.\]

The deterministic mean trend \(at\) does not remove accumulated random shocks. Subtracting \(at\) leaves a random walk. By contrast, a trend-stationary process \(z_t=b_0+b_1t+v_t\) has stationary weakly dependent deviations \(v_t\); removing the correct deterministic trend exposes those deviations. The lecture's “include a trend” reassurance on p.18 concerns that case and still requires valid regression assumptions. It is not a remedy for any series that rises over time. Persistence can exist without a visible trend (the lecture names interest rates, inflation and unemployment), and a trend can exist without persistent stochastic shocks. 2026_Lecture3_print.pdf, physical PDF p. 18, 28, 30 Wooldridge, Introductory Econometrics, 5th ed., printed p. 394 (PDF p. 422) Wooldridge, Introductory Econometrics, 5th ed., printed p. 395 (PDF p. 423)

Integration and first differences

In this course's framing, an \(I(0)\) process has the weak-dependence properties needed for ordinary limit-theorem reasoning; an \(I(1)\) level requires one difference to obtain such a process. For the random walk,

\[\Delta x_t=x_t-x_{t-1}=\varepsilon_t,\]

so differences are i.i.d. and \(I(0)\) even though levels are \(I(1)\). With drift the difference is \(a+\varepsilon_t\). General \(I(1)\) differences need not be i.i.d.; they may retain weak serial dependence. Differencing changes the modeled variable from its level to its period-to-period change, so coefficients must be interpreted accordingly. It does not automatically cure endogeneity or every inference problem. 2026_Lecture3_print.pdf, physical PDF p. 29 Wooldridge, Introductory Econometrics, 5th ed., printed p. 396 (PDF p. 424)

For a deterministic trend, \(\Delta z_t=b_1+\Delta v_t\), which also removes its changing mean but may alter short-run dependence. Choosing between detrending and differencing therefore requires a process argument; “both remove a visible trend” is insufficient. Online verification: Hyndman and Athanasopoulos, Forecasting: Principles and Practice, 3rd ed., §9.1 “Stationarity and differencing,” subsections “Differencing” and “Random walk model,” confirms the random-walk transformation and warns that unnecessary differencing can introduce autocorrelation. Consulted author textbook section. 2026_Lecture3_print.pdf, physical PDF p. 18, 29–30

Shared-looking movements in one observed history need not represent an economic connection.

03

Key definitions and concepts

  • Consistency: Convergence in probability of an estimator to the parameter.
  • Probability limit: The value approached in probability as sample size grows.
  • Chebyshev bound: A variance-based upper bound on the probability of a specified deviation.

3.1 Notation and distinctions

Consistency is \(\hat\theta_n\to_p\theta\): for every fixed \(\epsilon>0\), \(P(|\text{estimate}-\text{target}|>\epsilon)\to0\). It concerns a sequence indexed by \(n\), not monotonic improvement of every realized sample. Probability limit names the value around which the sequence concentrates; it may differ from the intended parameter.

04

Consistency: concentration as sample size grows

Consistency is convergence in probability: \(\hat\beta_{j,n}\xrightarrow{p}\beta_j\). More explicitly, for every fixed tolerance \(\varepsilon>0\),

\[\lim_{n\to\infty}P(|\hat\beta_{j,n}-\beta_j|>\varepsilon)=0.\]

The subscript \(n\) emphasizes a sequence of estimators indexed by sample size. Consistency says that the probability of an error larger than any fixed tolerance approaches zero.

It does not promise monotonic improvement for every realized sequence of samples. The lecture states OLS consistency under the first four assumptions and then shows that zero conditional mean is stronger than necessary for this particular large-sample result.

4.1 Why population covariance determines the probability limit

Divide both parts of the disturbance ratio by \(n\):

\[\hat\beta_1=\beta_1+ \frac{n^{-1}\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(u_i-\bar u)} {n^{-1}\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2}.\]

Under random sampling and finite moments sufficient for the law of large numbers, the numerator converges in probability to \(\operatorname{Cov}(x,u)\) and the denominator to \(\operatorname{Var}(x)\). With a positive finite population regressor variance, taking the ratio therefore gives

\[\operatorname{plim}\hat\beta_1 =\beta_1+\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(x,u)}{\operatorname{Var}(x)}.\]

Here \(\operatorname{plim}\) means probability limit. Finite moments and a nonzero limiting denominator are the regularity requirements needed to interpret the lecture's law-of-large-numbers step;

the slide does not spell them out. If \(\operatorname{Cov}(x,u)=0\), the additional term vanishes.

If it is nonzero, more observations concentrate OLS around the wrong value. A larger sample therefore cannot by itself cure regressor–disturbance correlation.

Source crop: Lecture 1, PDF p. 22. Centering and division by \(n\) turn the estimation-error ratio into sample covariance over sample variance, which exposes its population limit.

4.2 A weaker exogeneity condition

For consistency, the lecture replaces zero conditional mean by the weaker conditions

\[E[u]=0, \qquad \operatorname{Cov}(x_j,u)=0\quad\text{for each nonconstant regressor }j=1,\ldots,k.\]

The first condition handles the intercept, and the covariance conditions handle the slopes. The slide writes its index as \(j=0,\ldots,k\);

with a constant regressor \(x_0=1\), its covariance with \(u\) is automatically zero. This weaker condition, together with the other sampling, model, nondegeneracy, and moment conditions, supports consistency but need not imply finite-sample unbiasedness.

Zero conditional mean implies these unconditional moments, whereas unconditional zero covariance need not imply a zero conditional mean at every regressor value.

4.3 How unbiasedness and consistency can differ

The lecture states that an unbiased estimator is consistent if its variance tends to zero. Explanation: when \(E[\hat\theta_n]=\theta\), Chebyshev's inequality bounds \(P(|\hat\theta_n-\theta|>\varepsilon)\) by \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta_n)/\varepsilon^2\), so a vanishing variance implies convergence in probability. Unbiasedness alone does not control the variance.

Conversely, finite-sample bias can vanish as \(n\) grows, so a biased estimator can still be consistent. These are properties of the sampling sequence, not a classification that can be proved by examining one estimate.

05

Connect the reasoning

  1. Use \(\hat\beta_1=\beta_1+\frac{n^{-1}\sum(x_i-\bar x)(u_i-\bar u)}{n^{-1}\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2}\), obtained by centered substitution and common division by \(n\).
  2. Under independent random sampling and finite moments sufficient for the law of large numbers, sample covariance tends to \(\operatorname{Cov}(x, u)\), sample variance to \(\operatorname{Var}(x)\). Require positive finite population \(\operatorname{Var}(x)\) so the ratio is valid. Then \(\operatorname{plim}\text{slope}=\beta_1+\operatorname{Cov}(x,u)/\operatorname{Var}(x)\).
  3. If covariance \(0\) the slope limit equals \(\beta_1\); otherwise larger samples concentrate at the wrong target. Zero conditional mean is sufficient, stronger than needed for these moments. The weaker condition is \(E[u]=0\) for the intercept and \(\operatorname{Cov}(x_j,u)=0\) for every nonconstant regressor, alongside other model/sampling/moment/rank conditions. \(\operatorname{Cov}(1,u)=0\) says nothing about \(E[u]\).
  4. Finite-sample unbiasedness concerns an expectation at each \(n\). Consistency concerns concentration. Neither follows from one estimate or an unqualified large \(n\).
  5. For an unbiased sequence, Chebyshev bounds \(P(|\widehat\theta-\theta|>\epsilon)\leq\operatorname{Var}(\widehat\theta)/\epsilon^2\); if variance \(\to 0\), the probability bound \(\to 0\). Unbiasedness alone does not require variance shrinkage. A finite-sample biased sequence can be consistent if error/bias disappears with \(n\) and its sampling error concentrates; do not infer consistency solely from decreasing bias without controlling dispersion.

8.2 Question 2

Will more data alone remove that probability-limit discrepancy?

Reveal explanation

The large-sample limit itself differs from the true parameter.

8.3 Question 3

An unbiased estimator has variance \(4/n\). For deviation 0.5, what is the Chebyshev upper bound?

Reveal explanation

Divide \(4/n\) by \(0.5^2\). A bound exceeding 1 for small n is not a probability greater than 1.

Check your understanding

Correction: the LLN retains \(\operatorname{Cov}(x,u)/\operatorname{Var}(x)\); concentration needs a correct limit, while finite-sample unbiasedness alone gives no shrinking-error guarantee.
